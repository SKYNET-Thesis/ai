1.2 Project Overview
[A brief overview of the project, including the main parts that will be discussed in the document, to give readers a general view of the report's structure and content.]
This project investigates “whether replacing the original vision-language model (VLM) backbone of SmolVLA with an alternative lightweight VLM, and adding gated attention to its action expert, can improve robotic manipulation performance while preserving computational efficiency and accessibility”. We modify the SmolVLA architecture in two ways: (1) we evaluate an alternative VLM backbone with stronger visual understanding, and (2) we integrate gated attention, a head-specific sigmoid gate applied to the output of scaled dot-product attention, into every layer of the action expert (both self-attention and cross-attention layers). We also develop an adapter that lets other VLM backbones, which the original policy does not support, be swapped in easily. The resulting architecture is called SmollerVLA.
Our central hypothesis has two parts. First, a carefully selected backbone can improve task performance without sacrificing the model’s small size and speed. Second, the action expert can be improved further by gated attention, a recent mechanism that adds non-linearity and input-dependent sparsity to attention outputs, at a small computational cost.
To test this hypothesis, we run experiments on the low-cost SO-ARM101 robotic arm using the LeRobot framework. The modified SmolVLA variant (using LFM2.5-VL-450M as the replacement backbone, with gated attention in the action expert) is compared against the original SmolVLA and Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT) on two tabletop manipulation tasks:
* Desk Stationery Decluttering (pilot task)
* Halogen Lampshade Tray Packaging (secondary task). This task is harder than the first and was proposed after a practical task survey conducted for robotic-arm applications.
We assess task performance and computational efficiency (parameters, FLOPs, and inference latency) to determine whether the backbone replacement and gated attention provide measurable benefits under practical hardware constraints.
This report presents the project from initial analysis to experimental evaluation. It begins with project planning and management, followed by an analysis of existing systems and relevant technologies. It then describes the proposed methodology and system design, including backbone selection, integration, data collection, and model training. Next, it presents the implementation and evaluation procedures, followed by an analysis and discussion of the results. Finally, it summarizes the main findings, acknowledges the project’s limitations, and identifies directions for future work.

2. Project Background
[Introduce the context and setting of the project. This may include why the project was chosen, which problem the project aims to address, and the importance of solving this issue. This helps the reader understand the cause and purpose of the project.]
Before 2023, robotic manipulation research was largely confined to large, expensive hardware, which created a significant barrier to entry for the broader community. The ALOHA system and the ACT (Action Chunking with Transformers) paper changed this by showing that fine-grained manipulation tasks could be learned with high success rates on low-cost hardware. This catalyzed community-driven development, including affordable teleoperation systems, large open datasets, and scalable learning methods. Key outcomes include LeRobot, an open-source framework covering the entire robot learning stack, and the SO-ARM101, an even more accessible robotic arm. As hardware becomes cheaper, policies like ACT are increasingly complemented by lightweight vision-language-action (VLA) models such as SmolVLA, which are designed to handle manipulation tasks more effectively [34], [6], [14].
Despite being compact and efficient, SmolVLA has limitations. Its architecture is already small and heavily optimized, so improving its performance by simply adding capacity would undermine the efficiency that defines it. This is the trade-off between efficiency and model capacity. SmolVLA was built to make VLA-based control more accessible by reducing training and inference costs, using compact pretrained components and an open-source ecosystem. A compact backbone, however, may also limit the representational capacity available for visually demanding manipulation tasks. The SmolVLA paper itself raises this in its limitations section 5.1, noting that exploring how such architectures can scale without sacrificing speed or accessibility is an important direction for future work [26]. Two aspects of the architecture are worth examining.
The VLM backbone. SmolVLA uses SmolVLM2, which at the time offered a good balance of small size and moderate performance on video and image understanding. However, the SmolVLA authors explicitly note under “Choice of VLM backbone” that this model was pretrained mainly on document reading and OCR tasks, and that it is not yet clear whether it is optimal for real-world robotic interaction [26]. Such pretraining may be a poor fit for tasks that depend on object identification, localization, and scene description. This limitation reflects a design choice made to prioritize accessibility. It is not inherent to the overall SmolVLA architecture, which motivates testing whether a different lightweight VLM can improve visual understanding and downstream manipulation performance. Since the release of the SmolVLA paper in June 2025, several lightweight VLMs have appeared that are better or smaller than SmolVLM. After comparing candidates, we selected LFM2.5-VL-450M for its small size and its ability to process images effectively.
The attention mechanism. Recent work has shown that attention can be improved with small modifications, such as the gated softmax attention of the Forgetting Transformer and the gated attention of Qiu et al. . We ask whether a small addition of this kind can improve SmolVLA’s action expert at negligible cost. In this project we apply gated attention, a head-specific sigmoid gate on the output of scaled dot-product attention, in every layer of the action expert (both self- and cross-attention layers). Qiu et al. report that it adds non-linearity and input-dependent sparsity and improves training stability.
This project therefore pursues two interconnected objectives. The first is to improve SmolVLA’s visual-language capability by replacing its backbone and adding gated attention to its action expert. The second is to build an efficient adapter framework that allows alternative VLM backbones, which the original policy does not support, to be integrated easily. The work is not a plug-and-play swap. It establishes a systematic approach to backbone selection, integration, and evaluation, comparing the candidates’ architectural and computational characteristics [18], [29]. We call the resulting policy SmollerVLA, a smaller and better VLA than the original SmolVLA, with the aim of improving the performance–efficiency trade-off.
Using the low-cost SO-ARM101 and the LeRobot library, we evaluate SmollerVLA against two baselines, ACT and the original SmolVLA, on two tabletop manipulation tasks of different difficulty: Desk Stationery Decluttering and Halogen Lampshade Tray Packaging. The following sections describe the proposed framework, data collection, training configuration, experimental design, and evaluation metrics. The results are then analyzed to determine whether the backbone replacement and gated attention produce meaningful gains in manipulation performance and computational efficiency, and under what conditions.




3. Project Objective
[The specific objectives of the project. This includes what the project hopes to achieve at the highest level. Objectives should be clear, measurable, and achievable. This guides the development of the project and the evaluation of its success.]
The primary objective of this project is to investigate whether replacing SmolVLA’s original vision-language model (VLM) backbone with a lightweight alternative (LFM2.5-VL-450M), and adding gated attention to its action expert, can improve robotic manipulation performance while maintaining or improving computational efficiency. Two related concerns motivate this. The first is whether the original backbone’s visual representations suit manipulation, since the SmolVLA authors note it was pretrained mainly on document reading and OCR. The second is the trade-off between model capability, computational cost, and accessibility.
The project has the following specific objectives:
1. Design and implement SmollerVLA. Integrate the frozen LFM2.5-VL-450M backbone (first 8 language-model layers) into SmolVLA through an adapter that converts its features into the key-value inputs the action expert expects. Add head-specific sigmoid gated attention [Qiu et al., 2025] to every action-expert layer, and remove the original SmolVLM2 weights from the modified variant. Verify the implementation with sanity tests, for example that the gate set to 1 reproduces the baseline, and that the training path and cached inference path agree.
2. Train the baseline and modified models. Train 4 configurations on the same dataset with matching steps, batch size, and seed: ACT, the original SmolVLA, SmolVLA with LFM2.5 backbone and SmollerVLA. Record training loss, training duration, step time, and other cost indicators to support reproducibility.
3. Evaluate manipulation performance and computational efficiency. Compare the three configurations on Desk Stationery Decluttering and Halogen Lampshade Tray Packaging. Measure task success rate and efficiency metrics: parameter count (total and trainable), FLOPs, inference latency, and memory consumption. Determine whether SmollerVLA improves on the original SmolVLA and which configuration performs best under the defined criteria.
4. Demonstrate real-world applicability. Deploy the selected model on the SO-ARM101 and assess its suitability for halogen lampshade tray packaging at a partner factory producing halogen light bulbs. Produce a video documenting the system’s operation and assess its practical limitations under real operating conditions.
5. Develop a reusable backbone-integration strategy. Document the architectural modifications, compatibility requirements, implementation steps, and evaluation considerations of replacing SmolVLA’s backbone. Use the experimental findings to formulate an integration approach that can guide future backbone replacements and possibly other VLA architectures.
6. Assess the potential of lightweight VLAs. Determine whether SmollerVLA achieves a favorable balance between manipulation performance and efficiency. Discuss how far the findings support further development of lightweight, accessible VLA systems.




4. Problem Statement
[A specific statement of the problem that the project intends to solve. This section should detail the issue, including its causes, impacts, and specific challenges associated with solving the problem]
SmolVLA is a compact vision-language-action (VLA) policy of about 0.45B parameters. It is designed to be trained on a single GPU and deployed on consumer GPUs or even CPUs, while staying competitive with much larger VLAs on manipulation tasks. Its authors nonetheless identify limitations that this project targets [26].
Limitation 1: an off-the-shelf backbone that may not suit robotics. SmolVLA uses SmolVLM2, a general-purpose VLM pretrained mainly on document reading and OCR. The authors themselves say it is not yet clear whether such a backbone is optimal for real-world robotic interaction [26]. SmolVLA also keeps its visual input minimal: it disables image tiling and uses only the global image with pixel shuffle, which limits the input to 64 visual tokens per frame [26]. This may sacrifice fine detail on small objects, so perception and localization in cluttered scenes are a plausible weak point. This is a hypothesis that our experiments should test, not a result reported by the authors.
Limitation 2: little room to improve without adding weight. SmolVLA already uses a very small VLM and only its first 16 layers. The authors list scaling these architectures without sacrificing speed or accessibility as an open direction [26]. Gains therefore cannot come from simply making the model larger without giving up the efficiency that defines it.
Why these limitations exist. When SmolVLA was released (June 2025), SmolVLM2 was among the most compact VLMs with reasonable accuracy, which suited the project’s goal of accessibility. Under a tight efficiency budget, a small general-purpose VLM was a sensible choice, and the paper does not claim it was optimal.
What has changed. Newer compact VLMs now offer an alternative. We selected LFM2.5-VL-450M for its small size, its image-processing ability, and its hybrid convolution-attention language model, which keeps the early layers cheap. Separately, recent work shows that small attention modifications can improve training stability and quality at negligible cost, such as gated attention. This suggests that the action expert can be improved without enlarging it.
Problem statement. Can a carefully selected alternative VLM backbone, combined with gated attention in the action expert, improve SmolVLA’s manipulation performance while preserving its computational efficiency and practical deployability?
Challenges.
1. Backbone selection. The candidate must stay lightweight, and its pretraining should emphasize natural-image understanding rather than documents.
2. Layer selection. SmolVLA uses only part of its VLM’s layers, so the number of layers kept must be re-determined for each backbone. LFM2.5-VL mixes convolution and attention layers, which complicates this. We keep the first 9 of its 16 layers (the same half-depth rule as SmolVLA) [state whether you tested other depths].
3. Integration. SmolVLA’s action expert cross-attends to key-value features whose shape and statistics were learned from SmolVLM2. There is no tested way to swap the backbone, so we build an adapter that maps the frozen LFM features, plus the robot state, to per-layer key-value inputs for the expert. We also build the training entry point and verify the system with sanity tests.
4. Preserving the pretrained expert. Inserting gates into a pretrained expert risks destroying its behavior at the start of training. We initialize the gates close to identity (bias 4.0, sigmoid ≈ 0.98) and verify that a gate fixed at 1 reproduces the baseline.
5. Uncertain outcome. A new backbone or gate may not remove either limitation. Conclusions rest on training each variant under matched settings and comparing it with the original SmolVLA and ACT on two real tabletop tasks, measuring both success rate and efficiency (parameters, FLOPs, latency, memory).


5. Significance of the Project
[The importance of the project. This is where you describe the benefits that the project brings, both theoretically and practically, and its impact on the scientific community, industry, or society at large.]
SmolVLA was created to make vision-language-action (VLA) research more accessible. Most VLAs have billions of parameters and are trained on academic or industrial datasets [26]. SmolVLA is a compact policy trained on community-collected data. It can be trained on a single GPU and deployed on consumer hardware, while remaining competitive with much larger models on manipulation tasks [26]. The limitations described in Section 5 of its paper restrict how far this approach can go. These are a backbone pretrained mainly on document reading and OCR, and little room to improve without adding weight. This project addresses them, and its significance lies in three areas.
Scientific significance. Much recent VLA work improves performance by adding parameters, data, or compute. Far less work asks how a policy that is already lightweight can be improved while staying lightweight. To the best of our knowledge, few studies examine this question, and none we are aware of combines a backbone replacement with an attention modification in a policy of SmolVLA’s size. This project provides an empirical answer to two open questions:
1. The SmolVLA authors ask whether a VLM backbone better suited to robotics than the original one is available. We test this by replacing SmolVLM2 with LFM2.5-VL-450M.
2. We test whether gated attention, a small modification that adds non-linearity and input-dependent sparsity, can improve the action expert at negligible cost.
Each variant is compared against the original SmolVLA under matched training settings. Whatever the outcome, the results are informative. A gain would show that backbone choice and attention design matter in lightweight VLAs, and a null result would show that these changes are not enough, which is useful guidance for future work.
Practical and industrial significance. The project is implemented and evaluated on the low-cost SO-ARM101 arm, with Halogen Lampshade Tray Packaging as a task motivated by a real factory setting. If SmollerVLA performs reliably, the results will suggest that affordable hardware paired with a compact policy can handle simple, repetitive packaging and sorting workflows. This could give small and medium-sized factories a cheaper alternative to industrial robots. This is a hypothesis for the project to test, not a claim it can settle. Two tasks on one arm will show feasibility under these conditions, but they will not show factory-grade reliability, and the report will state this.
Community and methodological significance. The most reusable result is the framework itself. It documents how to choose a replacement backbone, decide how many of its layers to keep, and adapt its features into SmolVLA through an adapter for training and evaluation. Today, swapping the backbone of a VLA requires custom engineering each time. A documented and tested procedure lowers that barrier. The adapter is currently validated on one backbone, so the report will state which parts are backbone-specific and which carry over to other backbones. Together with open tools such as LeRobot, this helps keep research on lightweight VLAs open to people who do not have large GPU clusters.


6. Project Scope & Limitations
[The scope of the project as well as its limitations. This includes what the project will and will not cover. This section helps set expectations and prevent misunderstandings.]
What the project will cover: (1) SmollerVLA design. We will replace SmolVLA’s SmolVLM2 backbone with one lightweight VLM, LFM2.5-VL-450M, and add head-specific sigmoid gated attention [Qiu et al., 2025] to every layer of the action expert. The resulting policy is SmollerVLA. Other candidate backbones (for example FastVLM or Qwen3.5-0.8B) are outside the tested scope, and the report will state why LFM2.5-VL-450M was chosen. (2) Swapping framework. We will build and document an adapter-based procedure for swapping the backbone. It covers choosing which layers to keep (the first 8 of LFM2.5-VL’s 16), converting backbone features into the key-value inputs the action expert expects, setting up training, and verifying the result with sanity tests. (3) Data collection. Demonstration quality depends on the individual operator and on hardware calibration, and we do not use simulation. We will therefore collect our own demonstrations on the SO-ARM101, following the experimental protocol of the SmolVLA paper, for two tasks: Desk Stationery Decluttering (pilot) and Halogen Lampshade Tray Packaging (secondary). (4) Training. SmollerVLA, the original SmolVLA, and ACT will be trained on the same data with matching steps, batch size, and seed, so the comparison is fair. (5) Evaluation. The three policies will be compared on both tasks on the SO-ARM101. We will measure task success rate, and efficiency in terms of parameter count, FLOPs, memory use, and inference latency. (6) Analysis. We will analyze which factors (the backbone’s pretraining data, its vision encoder, the layer choice, or the gates) appear to relate to performance differences, and report the limitations of the framework.
What the project will not cover: (1) Propose a completely new VLA architecture. The action expert is modified only by adding gated attention and by changes needed to connect the new backbone. (2) Large-scale pretraining on community datasets. (3) Cross-embodiment evaluation, long-horizon tasks, or tasks other than the two above. (4) Larger VLM backbones (above 1B parameters), or any model for which the efficiency requirement no longer applies. (5) Deployment engineering for production, such as safety certification, long-term reliability testing, or integration with factory systems. (6) Fully eliminating the two limitations described in Section 4. We aim to measure how much SmollerVLA helps, not to prove that it solves them.
Limitations of this project
* One backbone. Only LFM2.5-VL-450M is tested, so a better-suited model may exist that we do not try. The adapter is validated on this one backbone, and its transfer to others is untested.
* Limited data and trials. Our training data is small (<1000 demonstration for each task) compared with SmolVLA’s pretraining data, and real-world trial counts are limited by time. Small trial counts give wide uncertainty on success rates.
* Two tasks, one robot. Results come from two tasks on the low-cost SO-ARM101, whose payload and precision are limited. They indicate feasibility for light sorting and packaging tasks only.
* Compute limits. Hyperparameter tuning and training length are limited by available GPU resources (currently two T4 GPUs and a 4,000-step run), so reported performance may not reflect each model’s best possible result.

2.2 Risk Management
[Describe potential risks during the project execution and preventive measures or response plans for each risk.]
Risk management is conducted through the identification, assessment, mitigation, and monitoring of potential events that could affect the project's schedule, resources, or deliverables. The risks are evaluated according to their likelihood of occurrence and potential impact on project completion. Preventive measures are established to reduce the probability of occurrence, while contingency plans are prepared to minimize disruption if a risk materializes. Three major risks have been identified for this project:
Risk 1: Training delays and loss of experimental results
Training the selected Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models and their variants may require substantial computational resources and execution time. Training may be delayed by limited GPU availability, software or hardware errors, interrupted sessions, or unexpected termination before model checkpoints and experimental results are saved. Such incidents could delay subsequent activities, including evaluation, comparison, and report preparation.
To mitigate this risk, the team will use GPU-enabled Kaggle environments, subject to available computing resources and usage quotas. Both AI engineers will prepare their training environments and execute independent experiments in parallel where resource availability permits. Training configurations, logs, evaluation results, and model checkpoints will be saved regularly and copied to persistent storage.Training will be prioritized according to the project's experimental objectives, with sufficient time reserved for evaluation and analysis. If the available compute budget becomes insufficient, the team will prioritize the essential baseline and primary experimental conditions before conducting supplementary experiments.
Risk 2: SO-ARM101 hardware failure
The project depends on the SO-ARM101 robotic platform for data collection and physical evaluation. Hardware failure caused by component wear, mechanical damage, electrical faults, or accidental mishandling could interrupt these activities. Since replacement components or a replacement device may require international shipping, equipment downtime could substantially delay the project.
To reduce this risk, the team will inspect and test the robotic platform before data collection and evaluation, follow appropriate operating procedures, and maintain backups of the collected datasets and associated configurations. A second SO-ARM101 arm will be prepared as a replacement, subject to successful assembly and compatibility testing. If the primary arm fails, the team will switch to the backup platform and verify its operation before resuming experiments. If both platforms become unavailable, the team will prioritize analysis of previously collected data, model training, software debugging, and other tasks that do not require physical access to the robot. Physical evaluation will resume when the equipment becomes available, and any resulting limitations will be documented in the final report.
Risk 3: Team member unavailability
A team member may become temporarily or permanently unavailable because of illness or other unforeseen circumstances. Since the project involves interdependent activities, including data collection, model training, evaluation, and report preparation, the absence of a member responsible for a critical task could delay dependent activities and create an uneven workload among the remaining members.
To mitigate this risk, the team will document task procedures, experimental configurations, data locations, and implementation details in a shared project repository. Each critical task will have a designated backup member who is familiar with its essential procedures. Team members will maintain sufficient cross-functional knowledge to take over urgent tasks when necessary. If a member becomes unavailable, the team leader will reassess task priorities, redistribute responsibilities according to each member's capacity and expertise, and adjust the schedule where necessary. Critical deliverables, particularly data collection, baseline experiments, evaluation, and report completion, will receive priority over nonessential extensions.
These risks will be monitored throughout the project. If a risk materializes or its likelihood increases, the team will assess its effect on the schedule and deliverables, implement the corresponding contingency plan, and document any resulting changes to the project plan. This approach is intended to reduce avoidable delays and preserve the feasibility of completing the project's essential objectives within the available time and resources.

2.5 Change Management Process
[Describe the process that will be followed for any changes in the project scope, schedule, resources, or other requirements.] 
The change management process controls revisions to VISTA’s scope, schedule, resources, and requirements while preserving the validity of its experimental comparisons. Changes to the SmolVLA backbone, dataset, training configuration, or evaluation protocol may affect both manipulation performance and computational efficiency. Consequently, revisions must be assessed and documented before their outcomes are incorporated into the final comparison. The process follows established change-control principles: recording requests, assessing impacts, obtaining approval, updating the baseline, and monitoring implementation [1].
Purpose and scope. Controlled changes include the backbone shortlist, task definitions, demonstration quantities, training steps, batch size, learning rate, evaluation conditions, personnel allocation, compute resources, budget, and arrangements for factory testing. Impact assessment will reference Protocol freeze (12-Oct), Review 2 (27-Oct), Dataset v1.0 frozen (30-Oct), Training complete (20-Nov), Review 3 (TBC),[f] and Final submission (30-Nov). Changes after a relevant freeze require explicit versioning and consideration of whether previous experiments remain comparable. Formatting corrections, routine task coordination, and code refactoring that preserves computational behaviour do not require a separate change request unless they affect an approved baseline.
Integration with existing project management. The process operates through WBS 1.4, “Risk & change-control log.” Nguyễn Gia Phát, the PM, is responsible for updating change records and affected plans within one working day after agreement. Google Drive holds reports, research documents, and the change-control record; ClickUp tracks assignments and deadlines; Kaggle holds training code. Discord supports communication, while Google Meet supports online meetings. Changes will be reviewed during full-team working sessions on Wednesday and Sunday and discussed during the 1.5-hour online meetings on other days when necessary. Updates will also appear in end-of-day reports and weekly stakeholder communications.
Step 1 — Change request. Any member may raise a request based on a technical issue, resource constraint, or revised requirement; stakeholder requests enter the same process. The input is a description of the problem and proposed adjustment. The requester provides the current and proposed states, reason, supporting evidence, affected work package or milestone, urgency, and acceptance criteria. Phát assigns a Change ID and records the request by the next working day. The output is a registered request awaiting assessment, rather than permission to implement it.
Step 2 — Impact assessment. The request and current baseline form the inputs. Phát coordinates an assessment covering scope, schedule, cost, quality, risk, dependencies, and external commitments. Nguyễn Minh Đức assesses backbone integration and training implications, reviews configuration consistency and metric comparability; Nguyễn Duy Sơn and Nguyễn Lê Anh Quân assesses data collection, processing, and setup requirements. The assessment identifies additional work, possible retraining, effects on Kaggle GPU free quota availability, and expenditure against the VND 12,504,000 budget. The output is a classification and recommended action, prepared before the next full-team review where practicable. Incomplete assessments remain pending.
Step 3 — Approval. The proposed approval structure distinguishes internal adjustments, research changes, and changes affecting Kahoku Lighting Solutions Vietnam Co., Ltd. The PM decides minor changes; medium changes require PM and supervisor agreement; major changes affecting factory commitments additionally require factory-partner agreement. Decisions will be recorded as Approved, Rejected, or Deferred, with the authority, date, reasons, and conditions. This structure is a prospective governance rule; historical records will identify the people who actually agreed rather than imply retrospective supervisor or partner approval.[g]
Step 4 — Baseline update. Following approval, Phát updated the change record and affected Google Drive documents and ClickUp tasks within one working day. Relevant updates include the WBS, schedule, budget, risk register, dataset version, protocol version, and training configuration. Changes to Kaggle code will reference a saved notebook version and the Change ID. Affected experiment records will retain the dataset and configuration identifiers used for each run. The output is a traceable revised baseline, communicated through Discord and the existing reporting cycle before work proceeds under the revised conditions.
Step 5 — Implementation and closure. The assigned member implements the approved change and reports progress through existing meetings and task updates. Phát checks the evidence against the acceptance criteria at the next scheduled review after implementation. Outputs may include revised code, dataset checks, completed assignments, or updated testing arrangements. The request is closed only when implementation and consequential document updates are confirmed. Failed checks reopen the request or generate a follow-up request.

1. Overview of the Field
[Provide an overview of the field of study related to your project. This helps place your project within the broader context of that field.]
Robotics aims to relieve people of repetitive, tiring, or hazardous physical work by producing motion that is useful, reliable, and safe. Since the field took shape in the 1950s, it has drawn on mechanical and electrical engineering, rigid-body mechanics, control theory, and software. Any working system must connect planning software to the hardware that carries out motion. Fig. 1 shows the methods for motion generation, which are broadly categorized into two primary families:
* Explicit approaches build on dynamics, using precise descriptions of a robot’s rigid links and of their contact with the environment. 
* Implicit approaches treat motion as a statistical pattern to be learned from sensorimotor readings [9]. 
  

Figure 1. Taxonomy of Motion Generation Methods
Robot learning belongs to the second family. Its rise reflects how hard it is to derive accurate models of robot–environment interaction, and recent results in locomotion, whole-body control, and manipulation suggest that learned behavior can rival explicit modeling [10].
Learning-based methods offer several advantages over the classical stack. Visuomotor policies map sensor readings directly to actions, so the interfaces between separate perception, planning, and control modules disappear. Learned feature extraction readily absorbs different input modalities. Where dynamics are unknown or impractical to model, interaction data can replace an explicit model altogether. Robot learning can also draw on the growing supply of open robotics data, much as computer vision and natural language processing grew with large-scale corpora.
As illustrated in Fig. 2, two main classes of methods have gained prominence: Behavioral Cloning (BC) and Reinforcement Learning (RL). On physical hardware, RL is hard to apply because exploration is costly and potentially unsafe, dense rewards are brittle and task-specific, and episodic training needs dependable resets. BC avoids these obstacles by casting control as imitation of expert demonstrations. The learned behavior then inherits the goals and success criteria implicit in the data, and no hand-crafted reward is needed. This project lies within BC and covers its two main branches. Single-task policies, represented by Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT), are typically not language-conditioned and each performs one task. Generalist policies, represented by SmolVLA, are conditioned on language instructions and produce motion across many tasks. The two branches differ in spirit but share a training principle, since both reproduce trajectories from a set of demonstrations, and this common principle justifies treating them together. The application studied here is tabletop manipulation, in which a robot arm changes the state of objects in its workspace through dexterous motion.
  

Figure 2. Taxonomy of Robot Learning Methods


2. Historical Context
[Provide some historical background or developments in the field, especially those directly related to your project.]
BC has a long history. Pomerleau [22] framed it as learning a mapping from observations to actions, a problem that can be approached through ordinary supervised learning. For decades afterward, robotics data stayed small and bound to individual tasks. Each expert trajectory was tied to one robot platform and the conditions of one environment, and human experts often performed the same task in quite different ways. Pooling such data was therefore difficult, and combining demonstrations from different embodiments could even cause negative transfer when control strategies conflicted. The result was a generation of specialist policies that performed well on their designated task but did not carry over to new deployments.
Two developments loosened these constraints. The first concerned hardware. ALOHA and ACT [34] showed that fine-grained manipulation could be learned on low-cost, imprecise arms, which encouraged community-built teleoperation systems, open datasets, and the LeRobot library (see Fig. 3 for an overview of its end-to-end framework). The SO-ARM101 used in this project descends from that movement [6], [14].
The second concerned scale. Large-scale models in natural language processing and computer vision had shifted those fields from task-specific training to a two-stage pattern of broad pretraining followed by task-specific adaptation, and robotics researchers began to ask whether the same recipe could work for control. Early generalist efforts such as BC-Z [11], RT-1 [5], and RT-2 [36] were followed by open datasets, including Open X-Embodiment [20] and DROID [12], that made open-source models possible. OpenVLA [13], π0 [4], and SmolVLA [26] are the main examples (Kim et al., 2024; Black et al., 2024; Shukor et al., 2025). In parallel, decentralized collection by individual researchers and hobbyists, supported by LeRobot, has become a significant source of data. Generalist models have also grown smaller and easier to run on limited hardware, a trend that matters for robots deployed outside large laboratories.

3. Key Studies and Theories
[Describe and assess key studies, theories, and published works related to your topic. This includes both fundamental and applied research.]
The development of robot learning has progressed from task-specific imitation-learning policies toward generalist systems capable of understanding visual observations, interpreting natural-language instructions, and generating corresponding robot actions. Early approaches primarily focused on learning motor behaviours from demonstrations, whereas more recent Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models incorporate pretrained vision-language models (VLMs) to transfer semantic knowledge into robotic control.
3.1. Task-Specific Imitation Learning: Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT).
Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT) [34] represents an important development in demonstration-based robot learning. Rather than predicting a single action at each time step, ACT employs a transformer-based policy to predict a sequence of future actions, referred to as an action chunk. Its architecture incorporates a Conditional Variational Autoencoder (CVAE) [27] to model variations in demonstrated action sequences, while temporal ensembling combines overlapping predictions during inference. Action chunking reduces the effective prediction horizon and helps mitigate error accumulation during sequential execution.
Unlike VLA models, ACT does not incorporate natural-language instructions or a pretrained vision-language backbone. Instead, it learns a task-specific mapping from visual observations and robot states to motor commands using collected demonstrations. Its relatively straightforward architecture and demonstration-based training paradigm make it a useful reference for evaluating the benefits of language-conditioned robotic policies. In this project, ACT therefore serves as a conventional imitation-learning baseline against which the performance of SmolVLA-based approaches can be assessed [34].
3.2. The Emergence of Vision-Language-Action Models: BC-Z, RT-1, and RT-2.
The introduction of language-conditioned robotic policies marked a transition from learning individual manipulation behaviours toward developing more generalizable robotic systems. BC-Z demonstrated that robot policies could be conditioned on task descriptions, enabling a single policy to perform multiple manipulation tasks. Building on this direction, RT-1 [5] expanded robotic learning through large-scale demonstrations collected across multiple robots and tasks. Its transformer-based architecture processes camera observations and natural-language instructions to predict discretized robot actions. This demonstrated the potential of scaling both demonstration datasets and policy architectures to improve task generalization [11].
RT-2 [36] further advanced this direction by introducing the Vision-Language-Action (VLA) paradigm. Rather than treating robotic control as an entirely separate prediction problem, RT-2 adapts pretrained vision-language models, including PaLI-X [7] and PaLM-E [10], to generate robot actions within the same token-prediction framework used for language generation. By combining web-scale visual-language knowledge with robotic demonstrations, the model can transfer semantic understanding to manipulation tasks involving objects and concepts that were not explicitly represented in its robotic training data. This approach illustrates the potential of pretrained multimodal representations to improve robotic generalization and establishes the VLM backbone as an important architectural component of VLA systems.
3.3. Large-Scale Robotic Datasets and Open VLA Architectures.
The development of generalist robotic policies has been closely associated with the availability of large and diverse demonstration datasets. Open X-Embodiment consolidated robotic datasets from multiple institutions and embodiments, providing approximately 1.4 million trajectories for studying cross-robot learning and transfer. Its findings demonstrated the potential advantages of training policies on diverse multi-embodiment data rather than relying exclusively on demonstrations collected from individual robotic platforms. Similarly, DROID introduced a large-scale dataset of real-world robotic interactions, further expanding the availability of demonstrations collected in diverse environments [20], [12].
Despite these advances, access to large proprietary VLA systems and their training resources remains a barrier to independent research. OpenVLA addressed part of this limitation by releasing model weights, training procedures, and evaluation resources. It combines a pretrained vision encoder with a Llama 2 7B [28] language model, projecting visual representations into the language model's embedding space before generating discretized robot actions. Although this architecture makes large-scale VLA research more accessible, its parameter count and computational requirements still present challenges for researchers working with limited hardware resources [13].
3.4. Lightweight Vision-Language-Action Models: SmolVLA.
SmolVLA [26] addresses the computational accessibility challenges associated with large-scale VLA architectures. It introduces a compact and efficient framework designed to support robotic learning and deployment on more affordable hardware. The architecture combines a pretrained SmolVLM vision-language backbone with a dedicated action expert that generates continuous robot actions using flow matching. Several architectural optimizations, including visual token reduction, selective vision-model layer execution, and interleaved attention mechanisms, are employed to reduce computational overhead while retaining the benefits of pretrained multimodal representations [17].
Besides, SmolVLA introduces an asynchronous inference framework that separates action prediction from robot execution, improving responsiveness during real-world deployment. Its compatibility with affordable robotic platforms, including the SO-100 and SO-101, makes it particularly relevant to research conducted with low-cost manipulators. Nevertheless, reducing model size does not necessarily eliminate limitations associated with the representational capabilities of the underlying VLM backbone. The backbone determines how visual information and language instructions are encoded before being incorporated into the action-generation process. Consequently, its architecture and pretrained capabilities may influence the model's ability to distinguish objects, interpret spatial relationships, and understand task instructions [26].
3.5. SmolVLM2 -  Lightweight Vision-Language Backbone. 
SmolVLM2 [19] is a family of compact vision-language models developed to provide multimodal understanding capabilities with relatively low computational requirements. It supports visual and textual reasoning tasks, including image description, visual question answering, and document understanding. Its lightweight architecture makes it suitable for integration into resource-constrained applications, including robotic systems. However, most of it training data is for document reading and OCR tasks, which leads to it weakness in handle images. This raises the possibility of replacing SmolVLM2 in SmolVLA with alternative lightweight VLMs that may offer more suitable representations for robotic control while maintaining comparable computational efficiency [26].
This consideration motivates the present project to investigate alternative lightweight VLM backbones within the SmolVLA framework. Rather than developing an entirely new VLA architecture, the project examines whether replacing the original visual-language backbone with alternative compact multimodal models can improve task performance while maintaining computational efficiency on the SO-ARM101 platform.

The text branch tokenizes the caption and encodes it into a global text embedding of compatible dimensionality. The global embeddings support image–text matching, while the unpooled visual sequence retains a representation for each patch. Pretraining for spatial features. SigLIP 2 trains the vision encoder with objectives that operate on unpooled patch features. From the start of the standard recipe, a LocCa-based decoder cross-attends to those features alongside the sigmoid alignment objective. The decoder learns three tasks: generating an image caption, predicting a bounding box from a regional description, and generating a regional caption from a bounding box. These tasks supervise the association between language and specific image regions. The decoder is used during pretraining and is omitted from the released encoder models [SIGLIP2].
   
The standard recipe adds two self-supervised losses during the final 20% of training. In local-to-global self-distillation, the student encodes a crop and is trained to match a representation derived from the teacher’s full-image view. The teacher weights are updated using an exponential moving average of the student weights. In masked patch prediction, the student receives a view with some patch embeddings replaced by mask tokens, while the teacher receives the corresponding unmasked view. The student matches the teacher’s features at the masked positions. The first objective trains consistency across views, and the second supplies a learning signal at individual patch locations [SIGLIP2].
NaFlex input processing. NaFlex allows a single vision encoder to process different image resolutions and aspect ratios. Given a patch size P and a target sequence length Nmax, preprocessing chooses resized dimensions H′ and W′ that are multiples of P. The resize approximately preserves the input aspect ratio while keeping the number of valid patches within the token budget [SIGLIP2]:
(H′ / P) × (W′ / P) ≤ Nmax

If the sequence is padded to the target length, an attention mask excludes the padding positions from attention and pooling. b).1. Introduction to Transformer
The Transformer is a neural network architecture introduced by Vaswani et al. in 2017 in the paper Attention Is All You Need [1]. It was originally developed for sequence-to-sequence tasks, particularly machine translation. Unlike traditional Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, which process sequential data step by step, the Transformer relies on attention mechanisms to capture relationships between tokens and enable parallel computation. This approach improves computational efficiency and helps the model learn long-range dependencies within sequences. The original Transformer follows an Encoder–Decoder architecture, where the Encoder extracts contextual information from the input sequence, while the Decoder uses this information to generate the output sequence. Its architecture incorporates several key components, including attention mechanisms, positional encoding, feed-forward networks, residual connections, and layer normalization. Among these, Self-Attention, Cross-Attention, and Multi-Head Attention play essential roles in processing information, while later variants such as Grouped-Query Attention (GQA) aim to improve memory efficiency during inference.
b).2. Self-Attention
Self-Attention is a core mechanism of the Transformer that enables each token to capture contextual information by interacting with other tokens within the same sequence. It allows the model to learn relationships between tokens regardless of their positions, making it effective for capturing long-range dependencies.

For example, in the sentence "The cat is sleeping because it is tired," Self-Attention can learn the relationship between "it" and "cat," helping the model understand the context rather than treating each word independently.

Scaled Dot-Product Attention (SDPA) is the core operation of the Transformer introduced by Vaswani et al. [39]. An attention function maps a query and a set of key-value pairs to an output, which is a weighted sum of the values, where the weight of each value is computed from the compatibility of the query with the corresponding key. Every self-attention, cross-attention and multi-head attention layer in the models of this project, from the language models of the VLM backbones to the action expert, is built on this one operation.
Formulation. The queries and keys have dimension dk and the values have dimension dv. In practice the attention is computed for all queries at once, by packing them into a matrix Q and the keys and values into matrices K and V:

As shown in Fig 4, the product of Q and the transposed K gives the similarity of every query with every key. These scores are divided by √dk, and a softmax turns each row into weights that are positive and sum to one. The weights then multiply V, so each output is a weighted average of the value vectors.
 Scaled dot-product attention: MatMul of Q and K, Scale, optional Mask, SoftMax, MatMul with V (Vaswani et al., 2017, Fig. 2 left) 

Fig 4. Scaled dot-product attention (SDPA). Source: Vaswani et al. [39], Fig. 2 (left).
Why the scaling is needed. Vaswani et al. compare dot-product attention with additive attention, which computes the compatibility with a small feed-forward network. The two have similar theoretical complexity, but dot-product attention is much faster and more space-efficient in practice, because it reduces to highly optimized matrix multiplication. For small dk the two perform similarly, but for larger dk additive attention is better unless the dot products are scaled. The reason is that large dot products push the softmax into regions where its gradients are extremely small. If the components of a query and a key are independent random variables with mean 0 and variance 1, their dot product has mean 0 and variance dk, so dividing by √dk brings it back to unit variance and keeps the softmax in a range where it can be trained [39].
Masking. SDPA includes an optional mask. Entries of the softmax input that correspond to illegal connections are set to −∞, so their weights become zero:

Here M is 0 for allowed positions and −∞ for forbidden ones. In [39] this prevents a position in the decoder from attending to later positions, which preserves the auto-regressive property.
Cost. A self-attention layer has a complexity of O(n2·d) for a sequence of n tokens with representation dimension d, but it needs only O(1) sequential operations and connects any two positions with a path of length O(1), whereas a recurrent layer needs O(n) sequential operations [39]. The cost therefore grows quadratically with the number of tokens, so the number of tokens that enter attention is a main driver of the computation of a transformer.
Relevance to this project. The attention pattern of [39] appears directly in SmolVLA, the policy that SmollerVLA modifies. Its action expert uses interleaved layers, each block containing either a cross-attention or a self-attention layer. In cross-attention, the queries come from the action tokens and the keys and values come from the VLM, which is the same pattern as the encoder-decoder attention of [39]. In self-attention, the queries, keys and values all come from the action tokens, and a causal mask prevents an action token from attending to later ones, which is the masking described above. The SmolVLA authors report that causal self-attention outperforms bidirectional self-attention (74.5% against 67.5% average success on LIBERO), and that interleaving cross- and self-attention gives the best results [26]. This matters for SmollerVLA in four ways.
Gated attention. The gate we add to every layer of the action expert is applied directly to the output of SDPA. For each head h, a sigmoid gate computed from the input of the layer multiplies the attention output element by element:

The softmax weights are positive and sum to one, so without a gate every head must return a weighted average of its value vectors, even when none of the attended positions is useful. The gate adds a non-linearity after SDPA and lets a head reduce its output when the context is irrelevant. Qiu et al. report that this adds non-linearity and input-dependent sparsity and improves training stability. The gate is applied to the output of SDPA and leaves the scores, the scaling and the mask unchanged, so it can be added to both the self-attention and the cross-attention layers of the action expert without altering the structure of the policy.
Backbone replacement. In cross-attention, the keys and values that the action expert reads come from the VLM backbone. Replacing SmolVLM2 with LFM2.5-VL-450M therefore changes the source of the keys and values. The scaling by 1/√dk assumes components of roughly unit variance, so features with different statistics, passed through the adapter, can change the size of the attention scores and the sharpness of the softmax. This is a point where the design of the adapter matters, and we treat it as a hypothesis, not as a result.
Efficiency. Because the cost of attention grows quadratically with the number of tokens, the efficiency of SmolVLA depends on keeping that number small. SmolVLA reduces the number of visual tokens and uses only the first 16 layers of its language model [26]. The backbone that we select must keep to the same budget, and LFM2 uses grouped-query attention to reduce key-value traffic [2]. The gate adds only element-wise operations and a small projection per head, which grow linearly with the number of tokens, so its cost is small compared with the attention it modifies.
Common building block. Multi-head attention, grouped-query attention, cross-attention and gated attention all differ from one another only in how Q, K and V are formed and what happens to the output of SDPA. Understanding SDPA therefore gives the basis for each architectural change in SmollerVLA, and the attention layers of ACT [34] and of the backbones all rely on the same computation.

4. Technological Advancements
[Discuss recent or significant technological advancements in your field, and how they affect your project.]
Three developments have made this project feasible on a student budget. Low-cost open hardware lowered the price of collecting robot data. An open software stack unified how that data is stored, used for training, and deployed. Compact vision-language models (VLMs) then reached a size where they can serve as backbones for lightweight policies. Each affects a different part of the project, from data collection to the design of the model.
Low-cost, open robot hardware. Early learning-based manipulation needed expensive, precisely engineered arms. ALOHA showed that fine-grained tasks could be learned on inexpensive teleoperated hardware, and ALOHA 2 [1] later refined this approach. The SO-100 and SO-101 arms [14] extend it further. They use consumer-grade parts and 3D-printable shells, and the LeRobot authors put a single arm at roughly 225 euros, against about 21 thousand euros for the ALOHA bimanual platform. The arms come in leader–follower pairs. A person moves the leader arm, the follower copies the motion, and the recorded motion becomes training data. The same pair of arms therefore serves for both demonstration and deployment. This is the setup used in the project, and it is why a student team can collect its own task-specific demonstrations instead of relying only on public datasets [6].
1. An open software and data ecosystem: LeRobot. Hardware alone leaves a practical gap, because robot data has historically been scattered across incompatible formats and robot-specific control code. LeRobot [6] addresses this with a single library covering the whole stack. A shared Python middleware reads the leader arm and drives the follower, whether under teleoperation or under a learned policy. The LeRobotDataset format stores sensor readings, multi-camera video, and task descriptions in one schema and can be streamed without downloading the full corpus. By September 2025, over 16 thousand datasets from more than 2.2 thousand contributors had been shared in this format, and over half of those datasets came from SO-10X arms. The library also provides tested PyTorch implementations of ACT, Diffusion Policy, π0, and SmolVLA, with training scripts for each. Its inference stack separates action prediction from action execution, so a policy can run on a separate, more powerful machine and stream action chunks to the robot. In the authors’ real-world tests on the SO-100, this asynchronous scheme gave similar success rates to synchronous inference but shorter cycle times, although the test used only ten episodes per task. For this project, LeRobot is the common platform on which all four model configurations will be trained, run, and compared under identical conditions [8].
2. Generative action models and compact VLAs. Modern policies no longer predict a single action per step. ACT predicts short chunks of future actions with a conditional variational autoencoder. Diffusion Policy and later flow-matching models such as π0 and SmolVLA learn the full distribution of actions, which suits demonstration data where experts act differently from one attempt to the next. Within this line, model size has become a design variable. LeRobot’s measurements place ACT at about 52 million parameters and SmolVLA at about 450 million, compared with 3.5 billion for π0. In fp32, SmolVLA peaks at roughly 1.75 GB of GPU memory, while π0 needs about 13 GB. This makes a language-conditioned policy practical on a single consumer GPU and gives SmolVLA a clear role as the efficiency-oriented generalist baseline here [34], [8], [4], [26], [6], [17] .
3. Compact VLMs as backbones. The most recent change is in the VLMs that supply a VLA’s visual and linguistic understanding. According to Li et al. [16], VLM design has moved from separate vision and language towers, through projector-based adaptation of a pretrained language model, to natively multimodal trunks. Model scale has diverged along the way. Frontier systems have grown toward trillions of parameters, while a parallel line has shrunk to sub-billion models for edge deployment. Two members of this second line are central to this project. FastVLM [29] pairs a hybrid convolution–transformer encoder, FastViTHD, with a small language model and controls the visual token count through input resolution alone, avoiding a separate pruning step. LFM2-VL, available at 450 million parameters, builds on a hybrid backbone of convolution and attention layers Both target low latency and a small memory footprint, the same constraints SmolVLA was designed around [18], [2] .
These developments set the project’s boundaries. Open arms and LeRobot make it possible to collect data, train, and deploy within the team’s own resources. Flow-matching VLAs with small action experts leave room to change the backbone without redesigning the policy. The newer compact VLMs offer candidate replacements that differ from SmolVLM in pretraining data and in how they handle visual tokens. Their published results come mostly from image-question answering, document, and chart benchmarks, and FastVLM’s reported latencies were measured on Apple silicon. Whether these gains carry over to manipulation success and to inference speed on the project’s hardware has not been established, which is the question the experiments are designed to answer. The LeRobot authors also acknowledge that quantization and graph compilation, which would further cut inference cost, are outside the library’s current scope, so the efficiency figures measured here should be read as unoptimized baselines [6], [29].

4.1. Introduction to LFM2
Liquid Foundation Models 2 (LFM2), developed by Liquid AI, is a family of efficient language models designed for fast inference and reduced memory consumption. Unlike conventional Transformer architectures that rely primarily on Attention, LFM2 adopts a hybrid architecture combining Gated Short Convolution and Grouped-Query Attention (GQA).

5. Gate Attention[k] - nói là thuật toán mới hỗ trợ các attention tốt hơn
5.1. Introduction and Motivation
Gated Attention is an extension of conventional attention mechanisms that introduces a learnable, input-dependent gating function to regulate the information propagated through attention layers. While standard attention mechanisms determine how information is aggregated from different tokens, gating introduces an additional mechanism that controls the contribution of the aggregated representations to subsequent computations.
In conventional Scaled Dot-Product Attention (SDPA), attention weights are calculated from the similarity between Query and Key representations and normalized using the Softmax function:
Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(Q KT / √dk + M) V
where Q, K, and V represent Query, Key, and Value matrices, dk is the Key dimension, and M is an optional attention mask.
Before attention dropout, the Softmax operation produces non-negative attention weights that sum to one across valid, unmasked Key positions. Consequently, each attention head generates a weighted combination of Value representations.
Although this mechanism effectively captures relationships between tokens, it does not provide a separate mechanism for directly suppressing the aggregated output. Even when no attended position provides particularly useful information, the Softmax operation still distributes its probability mass across the available positions.
Gating mechanisms address this limitation by introducing an additional learnable function that modulates attention outputs.
Qiu et al. (2025), in Gated Attention for Large Language Models: Non-linearity, Sparsity, and Attention-Sink-Free, systematically investigated different configurations of gating within standard Softmax Attention.
Their study examined several possible gating positions, including the Query, Key, and Value projections, the SDPA output, and the final attention output projection.
The authors identified a particularly effective configuration in which a head-specific sigmoid gate is applied directly after SDPA and before the final output projection. This configuration is referred to as the G1 gating position.
Their experiments demonstrated improvements in language-model performance and training stability. Through further analysis, they associated these improvements with additional non-linearity and input-dependent sparsity introduced by the gating mechanism.
5.2. Architecture and Mathematical Formulation
Gated Attention preserves the fundamental computational structure of conventional Multi-Head Attention (MHA).
The input representations are first transformed into Query, Key, and Value representations using learnable linear projections. Each attention head then independently performs SDPA to aggregate contextual information.
The main architectural modification is the insertion of a gating operation after the attention aggregation and before the final output projection.
Conventional Multi-Head Attention
For attention head h, the Query, Key, and Value representations are computed through linear projections:
Qh = XQ WhQ
Kh = XKV WhK
Vh = XKV WhV
where XQ and XKV are the query-side and key-value-side input representations, respectively.
The attention output of head h is calculated as:
Oh = softmax(Qh KhT / √dk + M) Vh
In conventional Multi-Head Attention, the outputs of all attention heads are concatenated and transformed using an output projection matrix:
Y = Concat(O1, O2, ..., OH) WO
where H represents the number of attention heads and WO is the learnable output projection matrix.
Gated Attention Mechanism
Gated Attention introduces a learnable gating function that modulates each attention head's output.
For head h, an input-dependent gate can be formulated as:
Gh = σ(XQ Wg,h + bg,h)
The gated attention output is then computed as:
Õh = Gh ⊙ Oh
where:
   * XQ denotes the query-side hidden states used to calculate the gating scores.
   * Wg,h represents the learnable gate projection matrix for head h.
   * bg,h represents an optional learnable bias.
   * σ denotes the Sigmoid activation function.
   * Gh represents the gating scores.
   * Oh represents the original SDPA output.
   * ⊙ denotes element-wise multiplication.
   * Õh represents the gated attention output.
The Sigmoid activation function is defined as:
σ(x) = 1 / (1 + e−x)
Its output lies in the interval (0, 1).
Therefore, a gate value approaching zero strongly attenuates the corresponding attention output, while a value approaching one largely preserves it.
The bias term is included in the general parameterization presented here but is not a mandatory component of every G1 implementation.
Tensor Dimensions and Gating Granularity
Gating mechanisms can operate at different levels of granularity.
Omitting the batch dimension, let:
XQ ∈ ℝNq × dmodel
Oh ∈ ℝNq × dv
where Nq is the number of Query positions, dmodel is the hidden dimension, and dv is the Value dimension of each attention head.
For element-wise gating, the gate projection has the following dimensions:
Wg,h ∈ ℝdmodel × dv
Consequently:
Gh ∈ (0, 1)Nq × dv
If a bias is included, bg,h ∈ ℝdv is broadcast across Query positions.
In this configuration, each attention-output feature can receive a different gating value.
In contrast, head-wise gating generates a single scalar for each attention head at each Query position:
Gh ∈ (0, 1)Nq × 1
The scalar is broadcast across the feature dimension of the corresponding attention head.
Head-wise gating provides coarser modulation, whereas element-wise gating enables independent modulation of individual output features.
Qiu et al. (2025) investigated both configurations and reported that head-specific gating was effective across different granularities.
In their experiments, element-wise G1 gating achieved the strongest overall results among the evaluated configurations, while head-wise gating provided substantial benefits with fewer additional parameters.
Final Output Projection
After the gating operation, the modified attention-head outputs are concatenated and passed through the conventional output projection:
Y = Concat(Õ1, Õ2, ..., ÕH) WO
This produces an output with the same dimensionality as conventional Multi-Head Attention when the output projection is unchanged.
Importantly, G1 gating does not directly modify Query–Key similarity computation, Softmax normalization, attention masking, or the Value aggregation operation.
Instead, it introduces an additional modulation stage between SDPA and the final output projection.
This allows the gating mechanism to be incorporated into existing attention architectures without replacing the underlying SDPA operation.
5.3. Functional Properties and Reported Empirical Findings
5.3.1. Additional Non-Linearity
One important characteristic of Gated Attention is the introduction of additional non-linearity within the attention computation.
Conventional SDPA already contains a non-linear Softmax operation. Nevertheless, when the attention weights are held fixed and biases are omitted, the Value projection, attention aggregation, and output projection together define a linear mapping of the Value-side input.
Qiu et al. (2025) identify additional non-linearity in this Value-to-output pathway as an important factor underlying the effectiveness of output gating.
In G1, the aggregated attention features are multiplied by a Sigmoid gate computed from the query-side hidden states:
Õh = σ(XQ Wg,h + bg,h) ⊙ Oh
The resulting transformation combines attention-based aggregation with input-dependent multiplicative modulation.
The importance of this mechanism lies in the fact that the gate changes according to the input representation.
This does not mean that the transformation becomes non-linear in the Value representations when both the attention weights and gating values are held fixed.
Rather, the gating function introduces an additional non-linear dependence on the input representations.
This increases the flexibility of the attention transformation beyond the original fixed-weight linear Value-to-output pathway.
5.3.2. Input-Dependent Activation Sparsity
Another important property of Gated Attention is its ability to introduce input-dependent sparsity in the attention outputs.
In conventional SDPA, Softmax distributes normalized attention weights over available Key positions. Every Query position therefore generates an aggregated Value representation.
Gated Attention introduces an additional mechanism for controlling this representation after aggregation.
Because gating scores are calculated from the input hidden states, different Query positions can produce different attenuation patterns.
For example, a particular attention head may receive gating values close to one for certain Query positions and values close to zero for others.
The gate can therefore learn different attenuation patterns for different inputs, although the semantic meaning of a particular gating pattern must be established empirically.
In element-wise gating, individual dimensions of the attention output can also be modulated independently.
This results in a form of input-dependent activation sparsity, where some output features are strongly suppressed while others remain relatively unchanged.
Qiu et al. reported that sparse gating patterns were associated with improved performance in their language-model experiments.
However, this sparsity should not be confused with computational sparse attention.
Sigmoid values approaching zero suppress attention outputs but do not automatically eliminate the operations required to calculate those outputs.
Therefore, Gated Attention does not inherently reduce the computational complexity of SDPA.
5.3.3. Reported Attention-Sink Mitigation
Qiu et al. (2025) also investigated how gating affects the attention-sink phenomenon.
An attention sink refers to the tendency of certain token positions, particularly initial positions, to receive disproportionately large attention weights even when their information is not necessarily relevant to the current prediction.
This behavior has been observed in autoregressive language models using Softmax Attention.
Because Softmax normalizes attention scores into a probability distribution, attention weights must be distributed across available positions.
The G1 mechanism provides an additional pathway for attenuating the aggregated representation without requiring the attention weights themselves to become zero.
In their experiments, Qiu et al. observed that models trained with output gating exhibited substantially reduced attention-sink behavior compared with conventional attention models.
For example, their reported attention-map analysis showed a reduction in average attention allocated to the initial token from 46.7% in the baseline to 4.8% in the gated configuration.
These measurements were obtained from the language models investigated in their study.
They demonstrate an empirical relationship between output gating and reduced attention-sink behavior, rather than a mathematical guarantee that gating eliminates attention sinks in every architecture.
5.3.4. Reported Training Stability
Beyond attention-output modulation, Qiu et al. (2025) reported improvements in training stability.
Their experiments on dense and Mixture-of-Experts language models showed that incorporating G1 gating reduced training-loss fluctuations and improved robustness under several optimization configurations.
The authors also observed that gated models could tolerate larger learning rates in their evaluated settings.
One relevant property of Sigmoid gating is that its multiplicative coefficients remain bounded between zero and one.
Consequently, the gate can attenuate the immediate attention-output features without introducing multiplicative coefficients greater than one.
However, this property alone does not establish why training becomes more stable, nor does it guarantee improved optimization.
Training dynamics also depend on other factors, including the surrounding residual connections, normalization layers, optimization algorithm, and parameter initialization.
Therefore, training-stability improvements should be understood as empirical findings reported by Qiu et al., rather than universal properties of Gated Attention.
5.4. Application to Self-Attention and Cross-Attention
The G1 gating mechanism can be incorporated into both Self-Attention and Cross-Attention because the gating operation is applied after SDPA.
The primary distinction between these applications concerns the sources of Query, Key, and Value representations.
Gated Self-Attention
In Self-Attention, Query, Key, and Value representations are generated from the same input sequence.
Given an input representation X:
Qh = X WhQ,       Kh = X WhK,       Vh = X WhV
The attention output is:
Oh = softmax(Qh KhT / √dk + M) Vh
The gating function then modulates this output:
Õh = σ(XQ Wg,h + bg,h) ⊙ Oh
Here, XQ represents the query-side hidden states, which may be normalized according to the surrounding architecture.
The mechanism allows different token positions to regulate their attention outputs independently.
When causal masking is used, the original mask remains unchanged. Therefore, gating does not introduce access to future token positions.
Gated Cross-Attention
In Cross-Attention, Query representations originate from one input sequence, while Key and Value representations originate from another.
Given query-side representations XQ and contextual representations XKV:
Qh = XQ WhQ
Kh = XKV WhK
Vh = XKV WhV
The attention output is calculated as:
Oh = softmax(Qh KhT / √dk + M) Vh
The output is subsequently modulated using the query-side gate:
Õh = σ(XQ Wg,h + bg,h) ⊙ Oh
This allows each Query position to regulate the magnitude of the contextual information retrieved through Cross-Attention.
Unlike Self-Attention, the gating input and Value representations may originate from different sequences.
Nevertheless, the location and mathematical operation of the G1 gate remain unchanged.
Thus, G1 can be applied to both attention types while preserving their respective Query–Key–Value relationships and masking rules.
5.5. Computational Cost and Limitations
Although Gated Attention preserves the underlying SDPA computation, it introduces additional trainable parameters and computational operations.
For G1 gating, the additional operations consist primarily of a linear gate projection, a Sigmoid activation, and an element-wise multiplication.
For Nq Query positions, hidden dimension dmodel, H attention heads, and Value dimension dv per head, the projection cost of element-wise gating is approximately:
O(Nq dmodel H dv)
The element-wise modulation requires:
O(Nq H dv)
For fixed feature dimensions, both costs scale linearly with the number of Query positions.
In contrast, conventional attention-score computation depends on interactions between Query and Key positions.
However, linear scaling with sequence length does not necessarily imply negligible overhead.
The additional projection can introduce a meaningful number of parameters and operations, particularly when the hidden dimension is large or the attention sequence is short.
Head-wise gating requires fewer gate outputs than element-wise gating and can therefore offer a lower-cost alternative.
Furthermore, small gating values do not automatically skip attention calculations or reduce the size of the attention-score matrix.
Consequently, G1 gating should not be regarded as a computationally sparse attention algorithm.
Qiu et al. (2025) reported limited inference overhead in their evaluated language-model configurations. However, the actual computational impact depends on the model architecture, sequence lengths, gating granularity, hardware, and implementation.
Finally, the reported improvements in language-model performance, attention-sink mitigation, and training stability do not establish that Gated Attention will provide equivalent benefits in every application.
Its effectiveness must be evaluated under the specific architectural and experimental conditions in which it is applied.
Overall, Gated Attention provides a relatively localized modification to conventional attention by introducing learnable, input-dependent modulation of attention outputs. Its principal characteristics are additional non-linearity, adaptive activation sparsity, and the empirical improvements reported in the study by Qiu et al. (2025).

Gap 2: Limitations of the original VLM backbone for robotic manipulation
A second limitation explicitly acknowledged by the SmolVLA authors concerns their choice of vision-language backbone. SmolVLA adopts SmolVLM-2, an off-the-shelf lightweight VLM, as its visual-language component. Although this choice contributes to the model's computational efficiency, its original pretraining distribution may not be optimally aligned with the visual understanding requirements of robotic manipulation. In particular, the SmolVLA paper identifies the backbone's emphasis on document understanding and optical character recognition (OCR) as a potential limitation when handling visually demanding manipulation tasks, such as object identification and spatial localization [26].
To investigate this limitation further, we examined the original SmolVLM paper, "SmolVLM: Redefining Small and Efficient Multimodal Models," rather than relying exclusively on the observations reported by the SmolVLA authors. Its visual pretraining pipeline provides additional evidence regarding the distribution of training data [19].
For static image pretraining, approximately 48% of the image training mixture is allocated to OCR and document-related tasks. The remaining data covers general image captioning (14%), reasoning and logic (9%), table understanding (9%), chart understanding (12%), and visual question answering (8%). Meanwhile, the video fine-tuning mixture emphasizes information-seeking and visual-description tasks, with information-seeking data accounting for approximately 60% and visual description and captioning accounting for approximately 76% of the video data. Temporal understanding represents approximately 18% of this mixture [19].
These training distributions suggest that SmolVLM was developed primarily as a compact general-purpose multimodal model rather than specifically for the spatial reasoning and visual grounding requirements of robotic manipulation. Although its existing capabilities are sufficient to support SmolVLA's original design, its pretraining characteristics raise a concern about whether alternative lightweight VLMs could provide more suitable visual representations for downstream manipulation tasks.
Proposed research direction
The two limitations above motivate an investigation into improving SmolVLA without replacing its entire architecture. Instead of developing a new VLA policy from scratch or increasing the model's overall capacity indiscriminately, this project proposes retaining SmolVLA's existing architecture while replacing its original VLM backbone with carefully selected alternatives.
The replacement backbones will be selected according to several criteria, including parameter count, higher evaluation metrics, and suitability for integration into the existing SmolVLA architecture. In particular, the selected models should offer competitive visual capabilities while remaining sufficiently lightweight to preserve the computational advantages of the original policy.
However, replacing the backbone is not necessarily a straightforward process. Different VLMs may employ different visual encoders, language-model architectures, hidden dimensions, token representations, and layer configurations. Consequently, the original SmolVLA components cannot necessarily be reused without modification. This introduces an additional technical challenge: developing an adapter and integration mechanism that allows alternative VLM backbones to communicate effectively with SmolVLA's existing action expert.
Accordingly, this project investigates the following research question: Can a carefully selected alternative VLM backbone improve manipulation performance while preserving, or further improving, SmolVLA's computational efficiency?
To investigate this question, the project will integrate LFM2.5-VL-0.45B and FastVLM-0.5B as alternative backbones, producing modified SmolVLA variants for training and evaluation. Their performance will be compared against the original SmolVLA and ACT baselines on the SO-ARM101 robotic platform. Both manipulation performance and computational efficiency will be evaluated to determine whether backbone replacement provides measurable improvements without compromising the lightweight characteristics of the original architecture.

Necessity of the Project. The development of SmolVLA addresses an important challenge in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) research: making general-purpose robotic policies more accessible by reducing their computational requirements. Unlike many earlier VLA systems that rely on large-scale models, SmolVLA is designed as a compact and computationally efficient policy that can be trained and deployed using a single GPU. This design objective is particularly relevant to individual researchers, students, and institutions that do not have access to high-performance computing infrastructure. For comparison, OpenVLA contains approximately 7 billion parameters, while  contains approximately 3.5 billion parameters. Although these models demonstrate the potential of large-scale VLA architectures, their substantial computational and memory requirements can create practical barriers to local experimentation, particularly on consumer-grade computers with limited GPU memory, such as those equipped with 8 GB of VRAM or less. Consequently, computational accessibility remains an important consideration in the development and adoption of VLA technologies [26], [13], [4]. 
Nevertheless, the development of newer VLA architectures continues to introduce increasingly sophisticated models, mechanisms, and training approaches, as summarized in Table X. While these developments may offer improvements in general capabilities and task performance, they do not necessarily address the computational accessibility challenge. In particular, the models examined in this study generally retain substantially larger parameter counts than SmolVLA. This observation highlights a continuing gap between improving the capabilities of VLA systems and making those capabilities accessible under limited computational resources. Therefore, further investigation into lightweight VLA architectures remains necessary, not only to reduce hardware requirements but also to enable broader experimentation, reproducibility, and practical adoption within the research community.
Originality of the Project. Beyond the motivation to preserve computational efficiency, this project investigates whether the performance of a lightweight VLA policy can be improved through a more systematic selection and integration of its Vision-Language Model (VLM) backbone. SmolVLA employs SmolVLM-2 as its original visual-language backbone. However, the suitability of this backbone for robotic manipulation tasks should not be assumed to be optimal simply because it provides an efficient foundation. Different VLMs exhibit different capabilities, architectural characteristics, and computational requirements. Consequently, selecting an alternative backbone may offer opportunities to improve visual understanding and task-relevant representations while retaining the efficiency of the overall policy [26].
To investigate this possibility, the proposed project extends beyond conventional backbone replacement by developing an adapter mechanism to facilitate the integration of alternative VLMs into the SmolVLA architecture. Since candidate VLMs may differ in feature dimensions, visual token representations, encoder structures, and intermediate feature outputs, direct substitution is not necessarily feasible. The adapter is therefore intended to provide a more systematic integration mechanism, reducing the need to develop an entirely separate, model-specific integration procedure for every candidate backbone. Its effectiveness and compatibility will be examined through the integration and evaluation of selected lightweight VLMs. 
Furthermore, this project aims to establish an experimental framework for selecting suitable VLM backbones for lightweight VLA policies. Rather than evaluating candidate models solely according to their standalone visual-language capabilities or parameter counts, the selection process will consider their suitability for robotic manipulation, compatibility with the existing policy architecture, and computational requirements. The resulting configurations will be evaluated against the original SmolVLA and ACT baselines on the SO-ARM101 platform. This evaluation is intended to determine whether alternative backbone configurations can improve manipulation performance without introducing an unacceptable increase in computational cost.
Overall, the project's contribution lies in investigating a systematic approach to improving lightweight VLA policies through backbone selection and integration. By combining an adapter mechanism with an evaluation framework, the project seeks to examine whether improvements in task performance can be achieved while preserving the computational accessibility that motivates the design of SmolVLA.

Its integration with Hugging Face's LeRobot framework further supports its use in robot-learning workflows, including teleoperation, demonstration collection, and policy deployment.

SO-ARM101 Robotic Arm. SO-ARM101 is a compact, open-source robotic arm intended for accessible robotic manipulation and robot-learning research. Each arm has five articulated joints and one gripper, commonly described as five degrees of freedom plus a gripper. Its mechanical structure is designed to support tabletop manipulation while remaining relatively compact and portable. The open-source design also allows users to reproduce the hardware, replace components, and modify the configuration to accommodate different experimental requirements.
In this project, the original SO-ARM101 mechanical design is retained, while the servo configuration is adapted using alternative components that are locally available in Vietnam. This modification is intended to make hardware sourcing and maintenance more practical for the research team. As the resulting configuration differs from the standard reference design, the exact servo models, gear ratios, and other relevant specifications used in the constructed arms are documented in the following hardware tables.
The compact form factor and accessible construction of SO-ARM101 make it suitable for the tabletop manipulation experiments investigated in this project. In particular, the platform provides a physical environment for collecting demonstrations, training imitation-learning policies, and evaluating the performance of Vision-Language-Action models under real-world operating conditions.

Leader–Follower Configuration. The SO-ARM101 teleoperation setup, shown in Fig., consists of two separate robotic arms: a leader arm and a follower arm. Although they share a similar mechanical structure, their functions and motor configurations differ according to their respective roles in the robot-learning workflow.
The leader arm serves as the human-operated input device. During demonstration collection, a human operator manually moves the leader arm to perform the target manipulation task. The corresponding joint movements are transmitted to the follower arm through the teleoperation mechanism, allowing the follower to reproduce the demonstrated movements in real time. This process provides an intuitive approach to collecting robot demonstrations without requiring the operator to manually specify individual joint commands or program the complete motion trajectory.
The follower arm serves as the execution component of the setup. During teleoperation, it reproduces the movements of the leader arm, enabling the collection of corresponding robot observations and actions. These recorded trajectories can subsequently be organized into a dataset for training imitation-learning policies through Behaviour Cloning (BC). During policy deployment and real-world evaluation, the trained model directly controls the follower arm to execute the learned manipulation tasks. Therefore, the leader arm is primarily required during demonstration collection, whereas the follower arm is used for both demonstration execution and autonomous policy evaluation.

In terms of its physical operating capabilities, the SO-ARM101 configuration used in this project has an effective working reach of approximately 300 mm. Within this range, the practical operating workspace is approximately 100–280 mm from the robot base, where the arm can perform the intended tabletop manipulation tasks. Its estimated payload capacity is approximately 300 g at the maximum working reach. The follower arm is equipped with a gripper that has an approximate maximum opening width of 50–54 mm, allowing it to grasp relatively small objects such as cubes, pens, small bottles, and other tabletop items. These specifications provide a practical reference for determining the size, weight, and placement of objects used in the experiments.
Although the leader and follower arms share a similar mechanical structure and can use the same servo product family, their gear-ratio configurations are different because they serve distinct purposes. The follower arm uses six servos with an identical 1:345 gear ratio. This configuration prioritizes output torque, allowing the arm to support its own structure and manipulate objects during task execution. Since the follower is primarily controlled by software rather than directly manipulated by a human operator, the additional resistance associated with a higher gear-reduction ratio is less of a concern.
In contrast, the leader arm is manually operated by a human demonstrator during data collection. Using a high gear-reduction ratio across all joints would make the arm more difficult to move manually due to the increased resistance associated with the transmission. To provide a smoother and more responsive teleoperation experience, the leader uses three different gear ratios according to the requirements of individual joints. Specifically, joints 1 and 3 use a 1:191 gear ratio, joint 2 uses a 1:345 ratio, and joints 4, 5, and 6 use a 1:147 ratio. The lower reduction ratios on selected joints improve manual back-drivability and reduce the effort required from the operator when demonstrating manipulation movements. Meanwhile, the higher reduction ratio is retained at the shoulder-lift joint to provide sufficient torque to support the arm structure. This configuration balances manual operability with the mechanical requirements of the leader arm.
Another notable difference between the two arms is their end-effector design. The follower arm is equipped with a conventional gripper consisting of movable jaws, which physically interacts with objects during manipulation. In contrast, the leader arm uses a pistol-grip-like handle with a trigger mechanism instead of a conventional object-grasping gripper. The human operator holds the handle and pulls the trigger using their finger to control the opening and closing of the follower's gripper. This design allows the operator to manipulate the leader comfortably while controlling grasping actions through a familiar hand movement. Consequently, the two end-effectors are designed around their respective functions: the leader provides an ergonomic human input interface, whereas the follower provides the physical mechanism for interacting with the environment.

Suitability of SO-ARM101 for our Project.  The suitability of SO-ARM101 for this project is evaluated based on the target manipulation tasks, available resources, hardware accessibility, and compatibility with the selected robot-learning frameworks. The primary tasks investigated in this project are Desk Stationery Decluttering and Halogen Lampshade Tray Packaging. Both are tabletop manipulation tasks that primarily require a single robotic arm to identify, grasp, transport, and place objects at designated locations. Therefore, a compact single-arm platform is sufficient to support the intended experiments without requiring the additional complexity and cost of a bimanual or industrial robotic system.
One of the main advantages of SO-ARM101 is its affordability and open-source design. Given the project's limited financial resources and the team's relatively limited experience in mechanical and robotic hardware engineering, constructing and maintaining an expensive research-grade platform, such as the Franka Emika Panda, would introduce considerable financial and technical challenges. In contrast, SO-ARM101 provides publicly available assembly instructions, hardware designs[m], and community-developed tutorials, making the process of sourcing components, 3D printing, assembly, and initial configuration considerably more manageable. Furthermore, its integration with the Hugging Face LeRobot ecosystem provides native support for common robot-learning workflows, including teleoperation, demonstration collection, and policy deployment. This reduces the amount of time and effort required for hardware development, allowing the team to allocate more resources to the primary research activities, particularly VLA model integration, training, and evaluation.
Another important advantage is its compatibility with lightweight robot-learning policies. Algorithms and architectures such as Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT), diffusion-based policies, and SmolVLA can be trained and evaluated on the SO-ARM101 platform through the LeRobot ecosystem. This makes it a suitable experimental platform for comparing different approaches to learning robotic manipulation under relatively constrained hardware conditions. Additionally, its compact size and portability simplify transportation, workspace arrangement, and repeated experimentation. Its relatively small gripper is also appropriate for interacting with common tabletop objects, such as stationery items and small packages, which are relevant to the manipulation tasks investigated in this project.
Despite these advantages, SO-ARM101 also introduces several limitations that restrict the range of experiments that can be performed. As a low-cost robotic platform, its mechanical capabilities are considerably more limited than those of industrial or research-grade robotic arms. Its relatively short working reach, approximately 300 mm, and limited payload capacity restrict its ability to manipulate large, heavy, or distant objects. Consequently, tasks involving heavy lifting, large-scale object manipulation, or operations requiring an extensive working workspace are outside the intended scope of this project. Furthermore, the use of low-cost servo motors may introduce limitations in positioning accuracy, repeatability, and mechanical consistency, which should be considered when interpreting experimental results.
These limitations directly influence the selection and design of the project's manipulation tasks. Desk Stationery Decluttering and Halogen Lampshade Tray Packaging are selected because their object sizes, weights, and workspace requirements can be accommodated by the SO-ARM101 platform. Both tasks provide sufficient opportunities to investigate visual perception, object localization, grasping, and action execution while remaining within the mechanical capabilities of the hardware. Therefore, although SO-ARM101 does not provide the versatility or performance of more advanced robotic platforms, its affordability, accessibility, and compatibility with the selected software ecosystem make it a practical and appropriate choice for investigating lightweight VLA policies in real-world tabletop manipulation scenarios.
Servo configuration. The SO-ARM101 robotic arms used in this project are equipped with Feetech STS3215-C001 bus servos (Fig …) , which are metal-geared digital servomotors designed for robotic applications. Each servo operates at a nominal voltage of 7.4 V (with a supported operating range of 4.8–7.4 V) and provides a stall torque of approximately 19 kg·cm at 7.4 V. The motors use a TTL communication interface, a 25-tooth spline, and an integrated digital controller, enabling position control through serial communication. Each unit weighs approximately 70.5 g and is supplied with a 30 cm cable. These servos are used to actuate the joints and gripper of the SO-ARM101, providing the torque and controllability required for the project's tabletop manipulation experiments.

Hardware-to-Computer system. The hardware-to-computer system integration describes how the power supply, sensing devices, servo control modules, and robotic arms are connected to the host computer. In this configuration, the host computer functions as the central processing unit and communicates with the external hardware through the OTN-8121 7-in-1 USB hub. The USB hub is connected to the host computer through a USB 3.0 interface, which provides a data transfer rate of up to 5 Gbps and serves as the main communication path between the computer and the connected peripherals.
The power supply network begins from a 220 V AC mains source, which is connected to the 60 W adjustable DC adapter. The adapter converts the AC input into a regulated DC output within the range of 6–7.4 V, which is supplied directly to the two servo bus drivers used for the Leader and Follower robotic arms. This voltage range is compatible with the operating requirements of the servo bus control modules and ensures that both arms receive a stable power supply during operation.

Two ICON Q-20 webcams are connected to the hub to capture visual observation streams, with support for resolutions of up to 4K at 60 fps. One OV9732 USB camera module is also connected to the hub to acquire close-range visual data for computer vision and recognition tasks, providing a resolution of 720p at 30 fps and a 100-degree distortion-free field of view. In addition, the Leader and Follower servo bus drivers are connected to the hub to enable bidirectional communication between the robotic arms and the host computer.
The Leader SO-ARM101 is physically manipulated by the human operator. Its servo bus driver reads the joint angles and position feedback from the servos and transmits this information to the host computer. The host computer then processes the received motion data and sends corresponding control commands to the Follower servo bus driver. The Follower SO-ARM101 receives these commands through its servo bus driver and reproduces the motion of the Leader arm, enabling teleoperation and imitation-based control.

   2. Desk Stationery Decluttering Setup
The Desk Stationery Decluttering task is a tabletop manipulation scenario in which a robotic arm collects scattered stationery items, such as pens, pencils, and erasers, and places them into a designated storage container, such as a pencil case. In this project, the task is defined more specifically to provide a consistent and reproducible environment for training and evaluating robotic policies.
The experimental setup consists of four physical objects: three stationery items and one rectangular pencil case. The stationery items include a brush pen, a ballpoint pen, and an eraser, while the pencil case [n]serves as the target container. At the beginning of each trial, the three stationery items are placed at different positions within the SO-ARM101's working area. The objective is for the robotic arm to identify, grasp, and transfer all three items into the pencil case,[o] which is positioned at a designated location within the same workspace. The initial positions and orientations of the stationery items can be varied between trials to introduce spatial variation and reduce reliance on memorized object positions. The dimensions and physical characteristics of the objects used in this experiment are presented in Figure X.
Motivation and Real-World Relevance. The selection of this task is motivated by the everyday need to organize personal workspaces, particularly study desks. Stationery items are frequently used by students and office workers, but they are also easily left scattered across a desk after studying or completing daily tasks. Although collecting these items is a relatively simple activity for humans, it is repetitive and may be postponed when users have limited time or motivation. Survey findings provide some supporting evidence for this broader problem. According to the American Cleaning Institute's 2022 College Cleaning Survey, conducted among 500 US college undergraduates, 53% of respondents identified a lack of motivation and 50% identified a lack of time as factors preventing them from keeping their dormitories or rooms clean [cite]. Furthermore, its 2024 survey of 500 US college seniors and rising seniors found that 45% considered having a clean living space beneficial for concentrating on schoolwork, while 33% identified decluttering their homes as a habit they wished they had developed before college [cite2]. Although these surveys address general living-space cleanliness rather than desk organization specifically, they indicate that maintaining an organized personal environment is a relevant concern among college students.
From an application perspective, a compact robotic arm integrated into a desk environment could provide assistance with repetitive object-organizing activities. For example, a student could initiate a decluttering task after finishing a study session, allowing the robotic arm to collect selected stationery items and return them to a designated container. This concept represents a potential application of affordable desktop robotic assistance, particularly for small and repetitive manipulation tasks. However, the proposed experiment focuses on demonstrating the feasibility of this specific manipulation workflow rather than developing a complete autonomous desk-cleaning product.
The task also provides a meaningful evaluation scenario for Vision-Language-Action policies. The robot must visually distinguish objects with different shapes and dimensions, determine suitable grasping positions, coordinate its movements, and place the objects into a relatively narrow container opening. These requirements introduce several manipulation challenges within a controlled tabletop environment. In particular, the difference between the thin ballpoint pen and the wider brush pen allows the experiment to examine whether a learned policy can handle objects with different grasping characteristics. By varying their initial positions and orientations, the task can additionally evaluate the policy's ability to respond to changes in the visual arrangement of the workspace.
Existing Solutions and Positioning of the Proposed Setup. Robotic platforms and research projects show that tabletop object manipulation can be automated. The Rotrics DexArm is a commercial desktop arm for drawing, laser engraving, and 3D printing, with optional gripper, suction, and vision modules, but it targets multifunctional fabrication rather than open-source VLA research [cite]. Tabletop HandyBot is an open-source tabletop assistant that performs pick-and-place from user instructions using an AR4 arm, RealSense D435 camera, and vision-language components, with a BOM of about USD 2,300—demonstrating language-guided manipulation but at greater complexity and cost than the compact SO-ARM101 used here [cite]. Most directly related, CleanBot (2026 James Dyson Award) uses an SO-101 arm for table-clearing via human demonstrations and imitation learning, showing the potential of low-cost arms for everyday organizing [ciet]. This supports the practical relevance of the proposed task, though it also indicates that SO-101-based table clearing has already been explored. 
In comparison, the proposed setup adopts SO-ARM101 because of its affordability, compact form factor, and integration with the Hugging Face LeRobot ecosystem. Rather than developing a complete commercial desk-cleaning product, this project uses stationery decluttering as a controlled real-world scenario for investigating and comparing lightweight VLA policies. The setup allows the evaluation of visual understanding, grasping, and object-placement capabilities using inexpensive and commonly available objects. It also provides a reproducible environment in which different VLM backbones can be integrated into SmolVLA and evaluated under the same physical task conditions.

   3. Halogen Lamp Tray Packaging (Inspecting)
Besides everyday tasks such as tidying a desk, a main goal of robotics is to assist people in industrial production, especially with repetitive, heavy or hazardous work. To evaluate SmollerVLA on a task closer to real industrial needs, we chose a second task based on a factory visit. Through a personal contact, the team was introduced to a manager at Kahoku Lighting Solutions Vietnam Co., Ltd. (KLS), who invited us to tour the factory in Binh Duong province.
Company background. KLS is the Vietnamese subsidiary of Kahoku Lighting Solutions Corporation, a Japanese manufacturer of special-purpose halogen and discharge lamps, founded on 1 August 1927 and headquartered in Ishinomaki, Miyagi [JETRO]. Its lamps serve as light sources inside analytical and inspection equipment for the medical and industrial fields, including semiconductor manufacturing equipment [Ishinomaki city profile]. The Vietnamese subsidiary was established in 2012, and its factory in Dong An 2 Industrial Park, Binh Duong, opened on 7 June 2013 [local press]. According to the manager, a second production site was opened abroad so that supply to customers could continue after the 2011 Tohoku disaster. The lamp used in our experiment is the JFR 6.6A-40/3 [confirm model code against the datasheet or label]. We chose it because it is light and small enough for the SO-ARM101 to pick up.
Task selection. During the tour, the manager shared insights and recommendations. We saw that several production steps could be automated but are still done by hand. One example is washing tungsten filaments before assembly. Workers dip the filaments in a NaOH solution, which can burn skin, and clean them with acid to remove oil, which is why they wear protective gloves. Setting up a robot-arm experiment around chemicals would itself be hazardous, so we chose a safer step from the same line: packing finished lamps.
Task description. After the plug has been inserted into the halogen lamp, the lamp moves to the electrical-connector potting step. Potting seals the rear of a connector with a resin that hardens into a solid, which relieves stress on the terminals and cables and makes the connection more resistant to vibration and shock. At KLS, this step serves as a supplemental bond, so that the plug-to-lamp connection does not come loose during shipping or in the field. digikey
The worker in charge of this step takes a lamp from tray A and places it in the drying machine. Using a dispensing needle, the worker applies the bonding compound around the plug and the adjacent part of the lamp. The drying machine then speeds up the curing. Afterwards, the worker places the finished lamp, plug pointing up, into tray B. Tray B is a fixed metal tray, and it has a second purpose. It lets the lamps cool, and it separates processed lamps from unprocessed ones in tray A. The worker repeats this cycle until tray A is empty and tray B is full. 
Tray B is metal and must not be moved. It also has no holes or grooves to keep the lamps from knocking into each other, so the lamps cannot be carried onward in it. After cooling, the worker therefore moves all the finished lamps back into tray A and sends them to the next stage, inspection and packing. Fig. X shows the actual setup at the KLS factory, which matches this description.
Tray placement varies between runs, and manual handling is inherently stochastic, so the position of each lamp relative to tray B is different every time. This variability makes the step a suitable evaluation task for the SO-ARM101 running our proposed SmollerVLA, because the policy cannot rely on memorized positions.

Task formulation. One successful placement of one lamp counts as one task, rather than filling all nine holes. This keeps each demonstration short and prevents the policy from getting stuck in a long sequence. One episode is:

   2. It carries the lamp to the nearest empty hole in the tray.
   3. It places the lamp in the hole, and the episode ends.

Since the primary learning approach employed in this project is a form of Behavioural Cloning (BC), the policy must first be provided with demonstrations of the desired robot behaviour. These demonstrations are collected through a leader–follower teleoperation mechanism. During operation, a human demonstrator manually controls the leader arm by holding its pistol-grip-shaped end-effector, with a finger positioned on the trigger to operate the gripper. Once an episode begins recording, the demonstrator moves the leader arm to approach, grasp, transport, and release the target objects according to the task requirements.
Simultaneously, the follower arm reproduces the corresponding movements of the leader arm, thereby executing the demonstrated manipulation sequence in the physical environment. The resulting observations and robot actions are recorded as demonstration data for policy training. However, the follower's movements are not expected to match the leader's movements perfectly. Small discrepancies may occur due to differences in calibration and mechanical alignment between the two arms.
One additional limitation of this teleoperation arrangement concerns the feedback received by the human demonstrator. Since the leader and follower operate as separate mechanisms, the demonstrator does not directly perceive the physical resistance encountered by the follower when interacting with objects. Although this limitation does not prevent the collection of demonstrations for the current tasks, it may affect manipulation consistency and place additional mechanical demands on the follower's actuators during repeated interactions. Therefore, it should be considered when evaluating the reliability and long-term operation of the system.

Processing has three steps. Steps 1–2 clean the data and Step 3 engineers language features. State, action and video values are not modified; only episodes are removed and language fields are added.
Step 1: Basic cleaning (integrity). Any episode with missing variables, missing camera frames or a missing video stream is removed, because broken or incomplete files cause training to fail. Step 2: Removal of stationary and jerky episodes (behavioural quality). The Hugging Face LeRobot dataset visualizer’s Filtering panel is used to rank episodes. Step 3: Language annotation (feature engineering).    1. Prompt (task_aug). 5. Method
SmollerVLA is a variant of SmolVLA built to be lighter and better designed. SmolVLA has two main components: a vision-language model (VLM) that encodes images and language, and an action expert that generates actions. SmollerVLA modifies the policy in four places:
   1. VLM backbone replacement
   2. A backbone adapter, so other VLMs can be swapped in easily
   3. Gated attention in the action expert
   4. A reduced action-expert width
This subsection covers the first modification and the motivation for the second.
5.1 VLM Backbone Replacement
a) Motivation
SmolVLA uses SmolVLM2, which was among the most compact VLMs available when SmolVLA was released. The SmolVLA authors note that this backbone was pretrained mainly on document-reading and OCR tasks, and that it is unclear whether it is optimal for real-world robotic interaction [26]. The SmolVLM paper supports this: about 48% of its static-image pretraining mixture is OCR and document data.
Manipulation depends on identifying objects, localizing them, and understanding the scene. A backbone with stronger natural-image understanding may therefore give the action expert better visual features. This motivates replacing SmolVLM2 with a lightweight, more general-purpose VLM released more recently.
b) Selection Criteria
Candidates were compared on metrics reported in their own papers and model cards. We used three criteria that are common in VLM comparisons and relevant to spatial and visual tasks: parameter count, RealWorldQA, and SEED-BENCH
Parameter count. The backbone’s size directly affects the efficiency of the whole policy. The replacement should therefore be no larger than SmolVLM2 (about 500M parameters). The shortlisted families were Florence-2, FastVLM, and LFM2.5-VL, whose smallest variants have about 200M, 600M, and 450M parameters respectively. FastVLM’s smallest variant exceeds SmolVLM2 by about 100M parameters and was excluded on this criterion, leaving LFM2.5-VL-450M and Florence-2.
SEED-Bench. SEED-Bench evaluates the generative comprehension of multimodal LLMs, including their understanding of visual content. We used it to compare the two remaining candidates. The score for LFM2-VL is 63.62, against 62.2 for SmolVLM2. The 70.6 reported for Florence-2 cannot be compared fairly with the small models.
RealWorldQA. This benchmark measures understanding of real-world spatial and physical scenes through visual question answering. Florence-2 has no reported score, while LFM2.5-VL-450M scores 58.43 against 49.90 for SmolVLM2 (+8.53 points).
LFM2.5-VL-450M scores higher than SmolVLM2 on every metric we could compare, with fewer parameters. We therefore selected it as the replacement backbone. Figure X compares LFM2.5-VL-450M, LFM2-VL-450M, and SmolVLM2-500M across benchmarks, taken from Liquid AI’s official release page.

c) LFM2 Architecture and Integration into SmollerVLA
LFM2 is described in detail in Section III.4 and in its technical report, so this subsection covers only what bears on the integration.
Architecture summary. LFM2 is a hybrid decoder designed for edge devices. Its design was found by searching over quality, latency, and peak memory with hardware in the loop. Most of its layers are gated short-convolution blocks, with a minority of grouped-query attention (GQA) blocks. It also uses SwiGLU feed-forward layers, pre-norm RMSNorm, and RoPE with QK-Norm in the attention layers.
Why this suits SmollerVLA. Convolution-heavy stacks avoid the key-value-cache growth and memory traffic of attention-heavy designs. Per the LFM2 report, this gives lower latency and memory use at batch size 1 on edge hardware [2]. That matches SmolVLA’s design goal of low cost on consumer hardware. The report also names a limitation: long-range retrieval depends on the small number of attention layers [2]. These are the authors’ findings on their own benchmarks. Whether they carry over to robot control is what our experiments test.
Layer selection: first 8 of 16 layers. SmolVLA uses only the first 16 layers of its VLM. We keep the first 8 of LFM2.5-VL’s 16 language-model layers, for two reasons.
   1. Following SmolVLA’s half-depth rule. SmolVLA keeps about half of its backbone’s layers [26]. The reason is not stated in the paper. We hypothesize that half depth provides enough visual and language representation for action generation, but we have not tested this directly.
   2. Skipping the last attention layer. The first 8 layers end just before the next attention block. In a short test, keeping 9 layers improved action MAE by only 0.05 over 8 layers. The extra layer adds a few million parameters, which conflicts with our efficiency goal. We therefore kept 8 layers.

Our approach differs from a plain benchmark-driven swap in two ways. We chose the backbone using explicit criteria, and we built an adapter that lets the new backbone feed SmolVLA’s action expert. The real-robot experiments will test whether the swap helps.
LFM2.5-VL cannot simply replace SmolVLM2. The two backbones differ in layer structure, feature dimensions, and the statistics of their hidden states. The action expert’s cross-attention was trained on key-value features from SmolVLM2, so it cannot consume LFM2.5-VL features directly. The next subsection describes the adapter we built to bridge this gap. It is also the basis for the reusable backbone-integration procedure.
5.2 VLM-to-VLA Adapter
Problem switching between VLM in SmolVLA. SmolVLA couples its vision-language model (VLM) and its action expert through cross-attention. At each expert layer, queries Q are computed from the action tokens, while keys and values (K, V) are derived from the contextual features of the VLM [26]. This follows the query/key-value pattern of encoder-decoder attention [39] (Section III.3c). The expert therefore does not consume arbitrary hidden states. It reads, at every layer, key-value tensors with a fixed number of key-value heads and a fixed head dimension. In the original policy, SmolVLM2 and the action expert are built together, so these tensors are produced by construction.
The original policy is designed around a single backbone, and its architecture does not provide a mechanism for replacing it. The problem addressed here arises when the VLM backbone of SmolVLA is changed. Replacing SmolVLM2 with LFM2.5-VL-450M breaks the interface between the two components for three reasons.
Dimensional mismatch. Let X_S ∈ ℝ^(B×N×D_s) denote the features of the original backbone and X_L ∈ ℝ^(B×N×D_l) those of the replacement, where B is the batch size, N the number of context tokens, and D the hidden dimension. The hidden sizes need not agree with each other, nor with the key-value dimension the expert expects.
Distributional mismatch. Even if the dimensions coincide, the two backbones produce features with different statistics and different semantic organization. The cross-attention of the expert was shaped around the features of SmolVLM2. As discussed in Section III.3c, the 1/√d_k scaling of attention assumes components of roughly unit variance, so features with different statistics may change the magnitude of the attention scores and the sharpness of the softmax. We treat this as a hypothesis rather than an established result.
Interface mismatch. The expert receives its context as a per-layer key-value cache. LFM2.5-VL has its own image-processing pipeline and a hybrid convolution-attention language model, and it does not natively produce a cache of this form.
No tested procedure for swapping the backbone of a VLA policy is available (Section I.4). Backbone replacement would otherwise require custom engineering for each new model. This work therefore treats the translation between a new backbone and the action expert as a design problem in its own right. To the best of our knowledge, no existing work addresses it for a policy of SmolVLA’s size, and we regard the adapter described below as an original contribution of this project.
Solution. We propose a trainable adapter placed between the frozen LFM2.5-VL backbone and the action expert. The adapter takes the contextual features of the backbone together with the robot state, and outputs per-layer key and value tensors in the format that the expert expects. This design is suitable for four reasons.
   1. The expert interface is unchanged. The expert still reads a key-value cache, so the policy does not need to be redesigned and the modification stays within the project’s scope.
   2. The backbone remains frozen. Only the adapter is trained on the backbone side, which limits the number of trainable parameters and the training cost, in line with the efficiency objective.
   3. The change is isolated. The contribution of the new backbone enters through one well-defined module, which facilitates attributing performance differences to the backbone.
   4. Reuse is possible in principle. Adapting a different backbone requires only rewriting its feature-extraction stage while the key-value geometry of the expert stays fixed. This supports Objective 5. However, the adapter has been validated on a single backbone, and its transfer to other models remains untested (Section I.6).

Operating Principle. At inference, the adapter operates as follows. First, the frozen backbone encodes the three camera views and the instruction into contextual features. Second, the conditioner maps these features into the key-value space of the expert and appends the robot-state token. Third, the layer-wise gates and biases produce a distinct key-value pair for each expert layer from the same shared projection. Finally, the cross-attention layers of the expert query this cache, and the flow-matching action prediction is conditioned on it.
The key and value projection is shared across layers, and only the gates and biases are layer-specific. The adapter therefore does not learn fully independent projections for each layer. This is a deliberately lightweight design choice, and we describe it as such.
Expectations. We expect the adapter to satisfy three conditions that can be checked directly:
   * Interface correctness. The output shapes match the key-value configuration of the expert at every layer, and the training and cached-inference paths yield consistent outputs.
   * Trainability. Under matched training settings, the model trains stably and the training loss decreases.
   * Efficiency. The adapter adds a small number of trainable parameters and little latency relative to the backbone and the expert. These quantities will be reported in the efficiency evaluation.
Whether the adapter allows LFM2.5-VL features to support better manipulation than SmolVLM2 is the central research question of this project. With the adapter, the action expert receives a context it can consume. The expert itself is also a candidate for improvement. Its cross-attention and self-attention layers pass the output of every head forward unchanged, even when the attended context carries no useful information.         5.3 Gate Attention in Action Expert
Motivation. Every attention head in the action expert ends with a softmax over the attended positions. The weights are non-negative and sum to one, so each head must return a weighted average of its value vectors even when none of the attended positions is useful. This matters in SmolVLA’s cross-attention layers. There the action tokens attend to a context made of camera features, the instruction, and the robot state, and a given head may have nothing relevant to retrieve at a given denoising step. In both self- and cross-attention, the value and output projections are two consecutive linear maps, so nothing between them lets a head’s output depend non-linearly on the current query.
Qiu et al. studied this in language models. They compared more than 30 gating variants in dense and mixture-of-experts models. A sigmoid gate applied to the SDPA output, which they call position G1, gave the most consistent gains in perplexity and training stability. They attribute the gains to two properties: added non-linearity between the value and output projections, and input-dependent sparsity of the attention output. They also report that gating reduces attention-sink behavior, where the first token takes a disproportionate share of attention.
This project asks whether the same mechanism helps SmolVLA’s action expert. The evidence from Qiu et al. comes from decoder-only language models with self-attention. Applying it to a flow-matching action expert with cross-attention is an extension that the paper does not test. This is an opportunity and a new point for us to test it on SmolVLA’s action experts.

Gate Initialization. The gate bias is initialized to 4.0. Since σ(4.0) ≈ 0.982, a zero-valued projection term would produce a gate value of approximately 0.982. This initialization therefore biases the gate towards retaining most of the attention output at the beginning of training. However, the actual gate is computed from both the input-dependent projection and the bias, gₜ = σ(xₜW_g + b_g). Consequently, its initial value is not necessarily 0.982 for every token or feature dimension. 
This initialization provides a near-pass-through starting point rather than an exact identity mapping. The gate can subsequently learn input-dependent modulation through gradient-based optimization. As an implementation-level sanity check, setting all gate values to one should recover the corresponding ungated attention output, provided that all other operations and parameters remain identical (Objective 1). 
Distinction Between the Two Gating Mechanisms. The attention output gate described in this section should be distinguished from the adapter-level gate introduced in Section 5.2. Although both mechanisms use input-dependent gating, they operate at different stages of the architecture. The adapter-level gate modulates the key and value representations generated from the LFM backbone and robot-state information before they are supplied to the action expert. In contrast, the attention output gate operates within the action expert, modulating the result of its attention computation. They are implemented as separate modules and serve complementary functions: the former controls the contextual representations provided to the expert, whereas the latter controls how the expert uses the resulting attention outputs.

5.4 modification expert width
The fourth modification changes the capacity of the action expert. In SmolVLA, the hidden size of the expert is set by a width multiplier applied to the hidden size of the VLM. The multiplier controls how many parameters the expert has, and therefore its training cost and inference cost. It is the one setting that lets us lower the size of the policy without changing its architecture.

	The learning-rate schedule affects how comparable the runs are. LeRobot’s SmolVLA default decays over 30,000 steps with 1,000 warm-up steps. The notebooks set the decay length to the run length, and the number of training steps is not smaller than the decay steps, so the scheduler’s auto-scaling does not trigger and the 1,000-step warm-up is kept. In a 4,000-step run the learning rate therefore rises to 1e-4 over the first 25% of training, then follows a cosine to 2.5e-6 at step 4,000. In the 5,000-step no-gate run the warm-up is 20% and the decay is stretched over 5,000 steps. ACT, with no scheduler, trains at a constant 1e-5, ten times lower than the VLA peak. This is one reason the loss curves of ACT and the VLAs should not be compared directly.
Fairness between ACT and the VLA family is defined as equal data, split, batch size (32) and step budget (4,000), not equal hyperparameters. 7.1 Evaluation Framework

Each level answers a different question.

	Level D is primary because offline error is an open-loop quantity. In closed loop, small prediction errors shift the visited state distribution away from the demonstrations and compound [N2], and a given deviation matters more at the moment of grasping than in free motion. Offline losses also need not identify the best-performing policy [N3]. Levels A–C therefore explain and constrain the Level D result and do not replace it.
No force or torque metrics are used. The SO-ARM101 provides joint-encoder feedback only. Contact force, grasp force and slip cannot be measured directly. Section 7.5.5 therefore defines trajectory-derived proxies and labels them as proxies.
Notation. One window is one action chunk of T = 50 steps over D = 6 joint channels. p(t,d) and g(t,d) are the predicted and demonstrated actions in raw joint units, with predictions un-normalised using the training-split mean and SD. n is the number of valid, non-padded steps in the window. σ_d is the SD of joint d over the training split only. It is used only as a denominator and never as a fitted quantity.
A – Training-Time Validation
Flow-matching validation loss. The training objective is conditional flow matching [17]. For a demonstrated chunk a and Gaussian noise ε, the interpolant is x_t = tε + (1−t)a, and the network v_θ regresses the target velocity u = ε − a [26]. For reproducible validation, noise and time are fixed. For each window w there are K = 10 noise draws ε_k, seeded from the validation seed, episode id, window start and k, with t_k = (k+0.5)/K:
L_w = (1/K) Σ_k  (1/n) Σ_{valid t}  (1/D) Σ_d ( u_k(t,d) − v_θ(x_{t_k}, t_k, o)(t,d) )²
Windows start every 50 frames. The episode score is the mean over its windows, and the reported loss is the mean over E validation episodes, with SD over episodes and SE = SD/√E. Only the six real action dimensions are scored, and the loss is computed on normalised actions.
Purpose. It selects the single best checkpoint, flags failed training, and compares checkpoints within one architecture.
Limitations.
   * The validation grid is uniform in t, while training samples t from SmolVLA’s Beta-shaped distribution. The loss is therefore a monitoring statistic, not an unbiased estimate of the training objective.
   * It is not comparable across model families, because the quantities differ.
   * It is not guaranteed to track task success [N3].
Masked L1 validation loss (ACT). ACT inference is deterministic, with the CVAE latent set to z = 0 [34]. Its validation loss is the masked mean absolute error on normalised actions:
L_w = (1/n) Σ_{valid t} (1/D) Σ_d | â(t,d) − a_norm(t,d) |
It is aggregated by window and then by episode, and is used only to select ACT’s checkpoint. Its training loss is L1 plus a weighted KL term, kl_weight × KL(q(z|a,s) ‖ N(0,I)) [34], [27]. [BLANK: kl_weight value from the resolved config.]
Checkpoint rule and degeneracy flag. The checkpoint with the lowest validation loss is kept (strict improvement, tolerance 1e-9). A run is flagged degenerate if best > 0.8 × first validation loss, i.e. less than a 20% improvement. This 20% threshold is a project-defined heuristic, not a published standard. The first validation occurs after val_every steps, not at initialisation, so the flag is conservative. Because the minimum is taken over about 200–400 validation points on a small validation set, the selected validation loss is optimistically biased. The sealed test set is the only unbiased offline estimate.
Monitoring metrics on validation episodes. At every validation, the real inference procedure is also run on the validation episodes and the Level B metrics are logged (test_log.csv). This is for diagnosing training dynamics. The test episodes are never accessed during training, and the test stage runs once, guarded by a lock file.
B – Offline Action-Prediction Metrics
Procedure. Each test episode of length L contributes up to 9 windows at start frames unique(round(linspace(0, max(L−50, 0), 9))). Predictions use the real inference procedure:
   * VLA models: 10 Euler denoising steps from one noise draw seeded by (test seed, episode, window).
   * ACT: the deterministic z = 0 chunk.
Metrics are computed per window, averaged within the episode, and summarised across episodes. The episode is the statistical unit, because windows from one episode are correlated.
Accuracy metrics : 
   1. MAE is the average joint error in raw units:
MAE_d = (1/n) Σ_t |p(t,d) − g(t,d)|        MAE = (1/D) Σ_d MAE_d
It is the most interpretable accuracy measure and the least sensitive to outliers.
   2. MSE and RMSE penalise large errors quadratically:
MSE = (1/(nD)) Σ_t Σ_d (p(t,d) − g(t,d))²        RMSE = √MSE (per window)
They expose occasional large deviations, such as a mislocated grasp pose, that MAE hides. RMSE is computed per window and then averaged, so it is not the square root of the mean MSE.
   3. Normalised MAE (nMAE) puts the six joints on a common scale:
nMAE = (1/D) Σ_d MAE_d / σ_d
Joint variability differs widely (Table 3 of Section IV.3 lists SDs from about 15 to 48). Without normalisation the large-range joints dominate the average. nMAE is a project-defined normalisation. It is dimensionless and shares one denominator across all models.
Smoothness and continuity metrics. A policy can have low MAE and still command jerky motion, which stresses low-cost servos and can drop objects. Smoothness is measured by finite differences along the chunk axis, where Δ^k is the k-th difference over valid steps (requires n ≥ k+1):
jerk  = mean_{t,d} |Δ³p(t,d)|          accel = mean_{t,d} |Δ²p(t,d)|
jerk_norm = (1/D) Σ_d [ mean_t |Δ³p(t,d)| ] / σ_d      (accel_norm analogous with Δ²)
The same quantities are computed on the ground truth (gt_jerk, gt_accel). Jerk is the classical smoothness measure of human reaching, where minimum-jerk trajectories describe point-to-point arm movements [N10].
Smoothness ratio:
SR = jerk(pred) / jerk(gt)
SR ≈ 1 means the policy is as smooth as the demonstrator. SR > 1 indicates jitter. SR < 1 indicates a smoother-than-demonstration (possibly over-smoothed) output.
Limitations.
   * Raw jerk depends on movement duration and amplitude [N11], [N12]. Fixing the window length at 50 steps removes the duration factor, and the ratio to ground-truth jerk removes much of the amplitude factor, but this is a mitigation and not a guarantee.
   * Third differences amplify servo quantisation and encoder noise in the demonstrations. An SR near 1 can therefore mean “matches the demonstrator’s noise”.
Chunk-boundary jump (bjump). For a seeded pair of consecutive windows A (start s) and B (start s+50), taken from episodes with L ≥ 100:
bjump = ‖ p_A(T,·) − p_B(1,·) ‖₂
It is the L2 norm over the six joints, in raw units. It probes discontinuity at the join between consecutively executed chunks.
Limitations.
   * Both windows are conditioned on demonstrated observations at their own start frames. This is teacher-forced, not a closed-loop rollout.
   * The ground truth also moves between steps s+49 and s+50. Report the reference bjump_gt = ‖g_A(T) − g_B(1)‖₂ and the excess bjump − bjump_gt.
   * Joint units are mixed (the gripper has a different scale from the joint angles).
   * With n_seams < 100 the value is indicative only.
   * ACT is evaluated without temporal ensembling in this study.
Aggregation and uncertainty. For each metric, report mean, SD (n−1), median, 90th percentile and a 95% percentile bootstrap CI over episodes [N4], [N17]:
CI95 = [ P2.5, P97.5 ] of { x̄*_b },  b = 1..B,  B = 10,000
Each x̄*_b is the mean of E episode values resampled with replacement (fixed seed). With a small E, percentile intervals can be anti-conservative, and a BCa interval is an alternative [N17]
Between-model comparison. All models are scored on the same test episodes, so use a paired bootstrap on Δ_e = x_e^A − x_e^B. Non-overlap of marginal CIs is only a conservative screen. Results are broken down by task instruction, with the 10 worst and 5 best episodes by MAE. A warning is raised if SD(MSE) > 0.7 × mean(MSE), meaning a few episodes dominate.
Interpretation limits.
   * Demonstrations are multimodal (different object orders, grasp strategies). Teleoperated demonstrations of one scene differ across attempts. MAE against the single demonstrated chunk penalises valid alternative modes.
   * This penalty affects a stochastic sampler (flow matching, one draw) and a deterministic mode estimator (ACT, z = 0) in different ways. Offline error is therefore not used to rank paradigms on task success.
   * The VLA metrics contain sampler variance from a single seeded draw.
